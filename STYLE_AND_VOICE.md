## Voice exemplars

The voice this project is reaching for, established by author edits during the v3-to-v4 prologue revision:

> *Climbing up the last of the way to the top, her breath caught, seeing framed against the all-too-bright night sky a brown shape, bleating at the sky. She stayed back for a moment, Margot hasn't lifted her head like this, not ever, even when pa had rushed the herd away from a wolf pack last spring. He always said that if she was going to be a wolf's meal, she'd be damned (she wasn't supposed to know this word) if she were to miss her own.*

What this paragraph is doing — every drafting agent should be able to point to each of these in their own prose:

- **Body first.** The first thing rendered is bodily ("her breath caught"). Not "she felt afraid." Not "she was startled." The body, then the cause.
- **Specific named things.** Margot. Pa. The wolf pack last spring. None of these are explained for the reader. The reader rides along in the child's familiarity.
- **Mixed tenses for subjective time.** "Margot hasn't lifted her head like this" is present perfect inside a past-tense narration. The strict past-perfect "Margot had not lifted her head" would be grammatically tidier and would kill the voice. Memory in a child runs in present-tense slips. *Do not correct these.*
- **Reported speech retold by the child.** "He always said that if she was going to be a wolf's meal, she'd be damned…" — pa's words come through the child's grammar, not as quoted dialogue. This is the project's preferred way to bring family and home onto the page without scenes set at home.
- **Parenthetical asides in child voice.** "(she wasn't supposed to know this word)" — the kind of aside a child makes about adult speech. Use these.
- **Stakes carried in vocabulary choice.** "All-too-bright night sky" already names something wrong without explaining what. "Hungry and haggard Margot" tells us the goat has been up here a while, has been frantic, is not herself. One arresting adjective does the work of three explanatory sentences.

## General principles (from `PROJECT_PLAN.md` §3.8)

The implied reader is a fantasy reader at home in the *Frieren: Beyond Journey's End* / *Tower of God* register. The prose commits to:

- **Trust the reader.** Don't gloss what context can teach.
- **Interiority is welcome.** Long character thought and slow attention are features, not bloat — but every scene must justify its length.
- **Worldbuilding through implication and texture**, not exposition dumps.
- **No glossary, no apparatus.** If a term needs a definition, the prose should provide it through use.
- **Melancholy is on-brand.** Resolution is not promised.
- **Episodic legibility within a long arc** — each scene satisfies on its own beat while serving the larger architecture.

## Anti-patterns: narrator-commentary that masquerades as showing

A specific failure mode flagged during the v3 prologue revision: prose that *sounds* literary because of generic-narrator framing but is actually doing nothing. The narrator commenting on what kind of perception is happening, instead of being inside that perception.

Banned constructions:

- **"…the way a child notices things…"** and any variant. Children do not think about how children think. Replace with the child's actual perception.
- **"…the way a child knows the small rituals of an animal she has lived with…"** Generic-literary framing of intimacy. Show the rituals. One specific image of Margot, not a list of what kind of rituals exist.
- **"She had been told this in different ways at different ages…"** Narrator summary of background. Show one of the tellings — a specific moment, a specific gesture (pa's hand on the back of the neck, ma turning her face away). One memory does the work the summary tries to do.
- **"She knew the pattern of it…"** Narrator-commentary verb. Replace with the pattern itself, rendered as something she perceives or remembers.
- Any sentence beginning **"the girl noticed…"** when followed by an abstraction. Either render the noticing as the noticing, or cut the noticing and just show the thing.

The replacement principle: **interiority through action, observation, and named memory — never narration of how the character thinks.**

If a draft contains a sentence describing the character's *mode of perception* rather than the perception itself, the sentence is wrong. Cut it or rewrite it as the perception.

## Mixed tense as deliberate craft

The voice exemplar slips into present tense at a specific moment ("Margot hasn't lifted her head like this") inside a narration otherwise in past tense. This is not an error. It is voice.

Mixed tense is correct when:
- **Memory surfaces in present.** When a character remembers something they once thought, the thought may surface in present tense even in a past-tense narration.
- **A sensory or emotional moment is vivid enough to slip out of past.** When something happens vividly, the prose can briefly slip into present.
- **Retrospective gestures sit alongside in-the-moment narration.** "I would never have a word for what it did" alongside "I stood at the wall" — the conditional/retrospective and the simple-past coexist because the narrator is remembering and retelling at once.

Mixed tense is wrong when:
- It happens by accident, with no reason behind it.
- The slip pulls the reader out of the moment rather than deeper in.

Editing agents should not normalize tense without first checking whether the slip is doing voice work. If unsure, ask.

## Resolved voice questions

### 2026-05-01 — Child POV stance for fragments where a child is the focal consciousness
**Resolution:** In-the-moment close third, with brief retrospective gestures permitted ("she would never have a word for it," "later, when…"). The retrospective gestures imply the child grows into an adult who carries the moment without becoming an adult-narrator frame. The child does her own categorization-work in the prose ("a man, the girl decided after a moment, because she had to decide something") rather than being narrated by the author into adult categories.
**Reasoning:** Per author preference. Pure adult-frame felt too on-the-nose; pure in-the-moment lost the elegiac quality the world calls for. The hybrid keeps the immediacy while gesturing at what the child is becoming. Confirmed in the prologue draft (`writing/fragments/prologue_emergence.md`).

### 2026-05-01 — Withholding as a structural commitment for ambiguous-by-design fragments
**Resolution:** Where the author calls for "deliberate ambiguity" on a thematic question (e.g. whether an emergence is near an imitation gateway), the prose withholds *every adjacent specifier* — name, place, era, named identities — so the reader cannot triangulate the answer from incidental detail. Specificity returns at the level of sensory detail (the brown goat, the third wall, the cookfire smoke from the smith's house) so the prose still feels grounded.
**Reasoning:** If only the gateway question is withheld but the era is given, readers will date the fragment and infer. Withholding has to be patterned to work.

### 2026-05-01 — Pacing register for prologue-scale fragments
**Resolution:** Short declarative sentences for sensory immediacy and child consciousness; longer accumulating sentences reserved for the moments that overwhelm the child (the air folding; the knowing-without-thinking). Trust the reader to feel the rhythm without explanation. No adjective unearned.
**Reasoning:** Per *Frieren* register and the fantasy-writer skill's "rhythm is meaning."

### 2026-05-01 — Genders of gods in the prose
**Resolution:** When a child or non-theological POV first encounters a god, the POV character does the categorization-work themselves and the prose names the god by whatever pronoun the POV settles on, *flagged as a decision the character is making* ("a man, the girl decided"). This honors §4.4's commitment that gods may have varying or non-binary gender presentation while keeping the POV legible.
**Reasoning:** Per §4.4 of `PROJECT_PLAN.md`. The mortal categories that POVs use are partial readings of divine presence; the prose can render that without breaking close-POV.
