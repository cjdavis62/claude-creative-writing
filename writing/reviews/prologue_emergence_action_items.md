# Writing Group — Action Items & Questions
*Distilled from the full session on "An Emergence" (prologue, fifth draft)*

---

## PART 1: CHANGES THE GROUP WANTS TO SEE

Changes are listed from strongest consensus to individual voice.

---

### ✂️ TRIM THE ENDING
**Consensus — all six voices**

The draft runs past its own finish line. "They had words for everything but the changes" is the right final sentence. Everything after "She held me longer than she had ever held me" is diminishing returns.

**Specific sentences flagged for cutting or reduction:**
- *"None of them would be mine."* — Seraphine: redundant; we already feel this
- *"None of them would name what my ma had seen on me when I came home that night."* — over-explains what the mother's face already showed us
- *"None of them would name what walked beside pa four days later, when he came back from the high pasture and would not say where he had picked up the company."* — Marcus wants to keep this; everyone else would cut it (see Questions below)
- *"None of them would name the change in old Margot, who came down in pa's company too, but who never grazed the lower terraces again."* — Cian: most expendable sentence in the passage

**Try this:** After "She held me longer than she had ever held me," cut directly to "They had words for everything but the changes." Read it. See if it breathes.

---

### 🔧 FIX THE STAR-GAZING SENTENCE
**Flagged by: Seraphine, Cian, Pat**

> *"Pa would show me the constellations on my favorite nights, but I struggled to find any of the ones I knew yet."*

Two problems: "my favorite nights" is warmer than this voice has licensed itself to be, and "struggled to find any of the ones I knew yet" is slightly clunky and too explanatory.

**Suggested direction (Cian):** Something like *"The stars were out early, wrong-bright, none of them where I expected."* Or redistribute the sky-wrongness to a different image — the animal behavior is already carrying this weight.

---

### 🔧 FIX "TWINKLE"
**Flagged by: Cian**

> *"Far up over the mountain the first stars were beginning to twinkle as the light faded."*

"Twinkle" is a children's-book word and slightly off-register for this narrator. Replace with something plainer.

---

### 🔧 SLOW DOWN THE TREE-LINE IMAGE
**Flagged by: Cian**

> *"A few bent pines, leaning the way trees lean when nothing has been there to argue with them."*

Beautiful image — but it arrives in a rhythm that has been building short, fast fragments ("Less grass. More stone."), and its length kills the forward motion just before the emergence. Either move it somewhere with more room, or shorten the surrounding fragments so the rhythm matches the image's weight.

---

### 🔎 AUDIT THE RETROSPECTIVE INTRUSION
**Flagged by: Seraphine, Cian**

> *"the word was not in any language he could remember"*

The child couldn't know this. The retrospective adult narrator might — but the draft hasn't clearly established that the retrospective narrator has this kind of understanding. Either: (a) frame it explicitly as the adult's retrospective inference ("what I understand now is that the word was not in any language he could remember"), or (b) keep the child's version only ("the word was not in any language I knew") and withhold the divine dimension of this detail. Don't let the child claim knowledge she couldn't have without owning that the claim comes from the adult narrator.

## PART 2: QUESTIONS THAT REQUIRE YOUR DECISION

These are genuine choices — the group is divided, or the group raised a question only the author can answer.

---

### Q1. Where exactly does the ending land?
**Raised by: all six voices (split on how much to cut)**

The consensus is to cut — but how much?

| Option | What it keeps | Who favors it |
|---|---|---|
| **Cut to the final line only** | "They had words for everything but the changes." | Seraphine, Pat, Cian |
| **One bridge sentence + final line** | One sentence acknowledging the village's mythologizing, then the final line | Middle ground |
| **Keep the pa/god detail** | Keeps "what walked beside pa four days later" — establishes the god didn't disappear | Marcus |

**The real decision:** Does the reader need to know, in the prologue, that the god descended with the father and remained? Or will that be established clearly enough in the main narrative that cutting it here is safe?

---

### Q2. How much does the retrospective narrator know — and should she say so earlier?
**Raised by: Yael, Pat, Cian**

The adult narrator who says "the closest I have come, when someone has had to ask and I have had to answer" is a person with a full, specific relationship to this memory. She has been asked about it. She has developed strategies. She has lived with it.

Currently the retrospective frame does a lot of work in the body of the piece (deepening uncertainty, lending distance) but isn't clearly established in the opening paragraph — the reader doesn't immediately know *when* they are, or *who* is narrating.

**The decision:** Is the opening paragraph the right place to commit more firmly to the retrospective frame? A brief orienting sentence or two — not explaining, just establishing — could give the retrospective intrusions in the body more weight. Or is the current approach (sparse, impressionistic, the frame revealed gradually) correct?

---

### Q3. Is the cold reader sufficiently oriented?
**Raised by: Marcus (concern); Pat (reassurance)**

Marcus worries the gap between "strange thing on a mountain" and "a god crossed between planes" may be wider than the draft accounts for — that a reader with no premise knowledge might finish this prologue feeling moved but unsure what they witnessed.

Pat disagrees: the reader needs to feel "something inexplicable happened and the community can't name it," not cosmological precision, and the draft accomplishes that.

**The decision:** Do you want the reader to understand *specifically* that they've witnessed a god's emergence — or is the felt experience of inexplicability enough at this stage? If the former, is there one small signal (a word, a detail, a phrase in the village's subsequent mythologizing) that would close that gap without over-explaining?

---

### Q4. Is the witness's transformation the same kind as the protagonist's gateway-mark?
**Raised by: Amara**

The witness crosses the boundary during an emergence, is seen by the god, and returns changed. The mother's face registers a change that has no category. This rhymes with the gateway-mark mechanic.

**The decision:** Is the witness gateway-marked? Is this prologue showing us *how* the protagonist's village became the protagonist's village — a site of proximity-to-emergence — without naming that directly? Or is the witness's change a different kind of change? The answer affects how much weight the prologue carries retrospectively when the reader learns about the drain.

---

### Q5. Does the village's mythologizing need one more beat of specificity?
**Raised by: Amara**

The prologue says "they would all have different words" for what happened on the slope — but we never see even one of those words. The abstraction is probably correct for this stage. But:

**The decision:** When the main narrative reaches this village, or when the witness recurs as an adult, should the different mythologizing words be present? Is the prologue setting up a contrast the main narrative needs to pay off?

---

### Q6. Is there a rereader reward still available?
**Raised by: Yael**

A strong prologue works differently on a second reading — the reader who knows the full story finds signals they couldn't have understood the first time. Currently the main reread reward is the final line.

**The decision:** Is there one detail — the god's specific behavior, something in the mother's response, a word in the child's description of the emergence — that would carry additional weight for a reader who knows the full story? This doesn't need to be engineered now, but it's worth holding as a question as the main narrative develops.
