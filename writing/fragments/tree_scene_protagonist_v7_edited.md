The trunk had bent double at the hip, pale in the lower wood where sheep had rubbed against it. She had walked half a day past the last marking-stone to find it.

She had come prepared.

Her hand had been loose near the sword since the second day. Eleven days on this road — marking-stones, ridge wind, the ground under her feet. She'd stopped checking the back trail somewhere around the fifth day. Just the road after that, and the god she'd been tracking. By now it wasn't anger anymore. It was direction.

The figure under the tree was in range. She had used this approach on gods who'd had no warning — the quick close, the sword already committed before they understood what was happening. Most of them had been standing. Most had been surprised.

He was sitting.

She stopped four paces out, hand at her sword, and then her hand stopped. Sitting was wrong. She had come to spend everything she'd been carrying on something that could take it — something upright, something she could hold accountable. What she found instead was the slump of him against the roots. His hands lay open in the dirt. She could hear his breathing from here: thin and deliberate, the breathing of something that had already conceded most of its functions and was defending only the last few.

That was not what she had come for.

He raised his head.

She knew what gods looked like when they were still intact — the cold that moved through her when they turned their attention toward something, her hands at weapons before she had decided to put them there. None of that happened now. His face held the pain level, practiced, the way you hold something you've been holding a long time. His eyes found her.

She'd felt divine presence before. What she felt now was a wrong note in the back of her teeth, in the small bones of her jaw. A remnant.

She closed her fists around nothing. The nails went in.

"I know what you came here to do," he said.

"Then you know to be afraid."

"No."

He let it sit. The ridge-wind moved across the field and was gone. Then: "I've been here three days. Waiting."

"You don't know me."

"Come closer," he said.

"No."

He spoke then, low, barely over a whisper. The sound reached her jaw before it reached her ears — not loud, just *present* in bone the way silence isn't. She was three paces out before she registered the movement.

"I know what was done to your sight."

She didn't answer for a moment. "What do you know about it."

"You look at what people give their lives to and find no worth in it." A pause. "You've seen what it costs."

The nails went deeper.

"There are copies of the divine crossings at every holy site," he said. "Built from love. Not calculation — love. Mortals who watched the gods cross and wanted to honor the passage. They built copies so more gods might come." He breathed — she heard the cost of it. "The truest thing they had."

Her free hand was still closed.

She had the contempt ready. What came out was: "I know what devotion costs."

It came out wrong. Her twin surfaced — the door, the face behind it, the eyes that no longer registered her as real. She pressed her nails deeper.

"Yes." He watched her steadily, the pain managed behind it. "Not theory. Not distance." Another breath. "A crossing is coming. The last one. I need someone to be there."

"Why me?"

He was quiet. The ridge-wind moved and was gone again.

He began to speak.

"The place," she said.

He stopped. Looked at her with something in his face that was not surprise and not disappointment. Then he gave her only what she'd asked: not a name, not a road, but the sensation of standing in a doorway that faced both ways at once. She would recognize it when she was in it.

She understood. She held the shape of it.

"You won't know if I go."

"No."

"You won't know if I don't."

"No." He shifted slightly against the root and pain crossed his face and left. "But you'll know. And there are things you don't leave unfinished."

She had the argument ready. She turned and walked back across the field.

She was perhaps a third of the way across when the breathing stopped.

She registered it in her stride — a half-step already landing when the quiet arrived.

She did not stop. She did not look back.

Her fists were still closed. She opened them as she walked — small crescents of dried blood pressed into both palms, already cooling. She let the field wind cross them.

The marking-stone was ahead. Past it, the road.

Behind her: nothing. Just the field, and the tree, and the silence where the breathing had been.

She kept walking.
