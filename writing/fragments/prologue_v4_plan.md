## 1. What v3 got wrong (honest diagnosis)

The criticism is correct. v3 leans on a register I think of as "literary-narrator-commenting-on-child" rather than actual child consciousness. Specific failures:

- **"The girl noticed these things the way a child notices things, which is fully and without weight."** This is the worst offender. It's the narrator telling the reader what kind of perception is in play, instead of just *being* in that perception. A child does not think about how a child thinks. Cut every line of this kind.
- **"The girl knew the pattern of it the way a child knows the small rituals of an animal she has lived with — where the goat preferred grass, where the goat preferred to lie down…"** Generic-literary framing. Tells me the relationship is intimate without showing me a single specific intimate thing. The user's edit fixes this with one move: name the goat **Old Margot**, and let the relationship emerge from how the child thinks about her.
- **"She had been told never to cross the third wall. She had been told this in different ways at different ages…"** Narrator summary of the child's history. Should be in the body — what does she feel at the wall, what does she remember, what does her stomach do.
- **The home life is missing.** I never showed her wanting to come back with the goat. I never showed her dreading what it would cost not to. The user is right that her relationship to home should drive her forward without being explained.
- **No named family beyond "her mother."** The user's edit introduces **pa**, a herd, a remembered wolf-pack incident from last spring. The family becomes a real family with real history. v3 had only a generic mother figure.
- **The "way a child notices" framing is everywhere, including in the chickens/dog/cat opening.** Those animals should have names, or specific descriptors that imply names she knows. The smith's dog isn't "the smith's dog" to her — it's whatever she calls it.

The honest summary: I wrote competent literary prose. The user wants alive prose. Those are different bars.

## 2. What the user's own edits are teaching me about voice

The user-written paragraph at line 52 is the closest thing we have to a voice anchor for this fragment. Reproducing it for analysis:

> *Climbing up the last of the way to the top, her breath caught, seeing framed against the all-too-bright night sky a brown shape, bleating at the sky. She stayed back for a moment, Margot hasn't lifted her head like this, not ever, even when pa had rushed the herd away from a wolf pack last spring. He always said that if she was going to be a wolf's meal, she'd be damned (she wasn't supposed to know this word) if she were to miss her own.*

What this paragraph is doing that mine wasn't:

- **Body first.** "Her breath caught" — no narrator framing, just the body's reaction.
- **Specific named things.** Margot. Pa. The wolf pack last spring. The "her own" at the end is a half-quoted dad-saying. None of these are explained; they're known to the child and the reader rides along.
- **Mixed tenses for subjective time.** "Margot hasn't lifted her head like this" — present perfect, not past perfect. Present tense slips in because that's how memory works in a child's voice. We should not "correct" this. It's the voice.
- **Reported speech as remembered.** "He always said that if she was going to be…" — the dad's saying gets retold by the child, in her grammar, with her aside in parentheses. This is the technique the rest of the prologue should use to bring home/family on stage without scenes set at home.
- **Adult speech with child's parenthetical gloss.** "(she wasn't supposed to know this word)" — pure child-voice; the kind of aside a child makes to herself when remembering something she shouldn't have heard.
- **Stakes carried in vocabulary.** "All-too-bright night sky" already names something wrong without explaining what. "Hungry and haggard Margot" (line 54) tells me the goat has been up here a while, has been frantic, is not herself.

The plan for v4 is to bring this register across the entire fragment.

## 3. POV question: first person vs close third

The user wants to consider first person. Worth taking seriously. Some honest tradeoffs:

**First person ("I climbed the slope…"):**
- Closes the distance to zero. The voice is the child's voice without a narrator filtering anything.
- The retrospective gestures become first-person too — *"I would never, in my life, have a word for what it did"* — which is more direct and more haunting.
- Makes mixed-tense voice slips natural rather than risky.
- Sets a precedent: this prologue's voice is different from the rest of the novel (which has Thread A and Thread B both in close third, per `STYLE_AND_VOICE.md`). That's a feature if framed deliberately — *"the prologue is a remembered account, the rest of the novel is third-person live time"* — but a question if framed accidentally.
- The "they would all have different words; none of them would be hers" closing line works differently in first person — *"they would all have different words. None would be mine."* That hits, and arguably hits harder.

**Close third (the current mode):**
- Consistent with the rest of the novel.
- Already what's on the page; less work to push the existing draft toward the user-paragraph register.
- Allows occasional outside knowledge the child doesn't have — though if we're being honest, we haven't used that affordance and probably shouldn't.
- Risk: easy to slip back into narrator-commentary if not vigilant.

**My recommendation:** I'd like to do a **voice test** before committing — write the same ~200-word passage twice, once in first person and once in disciplined close-third (no narrator commentary, child's vocabulary only). Show you both. You pick which one feels right and we scale that voice across the full v4. This is one extra round but it stops me from drafting 1700 words in the wrong register.

If you want me to skip the test and just commit, my instinct is **first person** — the user-edited paragraph already wants to be first person, and the prologue framing as "a child's account, remembered later" gives a natural reason for it.

## 5. New canon to invent

### Margot the goat
- Old. Female. Specific. Has been with the family longer than the girl remembers.
- Has a place she lies down in the heat. Has a wall she scratches her horn on. Has a sound she makes when she wants water.
- Has, before, never gone the way the prints go. The girl knows this in her bones.
- *Open question for you:* should Margot survive the prologue? She is alive at the goat's last appearance in v3, head down to the grass. The girl leaves without her. We never see her fate. Three options: **(a) the girl never sees Margot again; the village finds her later or never;** **(b) Margot returns to the village days later, changed — quieter, attentive in a new way, an animal that has been close to a god;** **(c) Margot is dead by morning, found on the slope, and what killed her is not specified.** I have no strong preference. Each does different thematic work.

### Pa
- Father. Has a herd (so the family is a herding family — at least small-scale, multiple animals). Wry. Gallows humor. Has been close enough to wolves to "rush the herd away from a wolf pack last spring."
- Has a saying about Margot. Probably has other sayings. Is a real person whose voice the child remembers.
- *Open question:* is pa present in the prologue, or out with the herd / elsewhere? In v3 only the mother is on stage. In v4 he can be either offstage (out with the herd, why the girl had to go alone) or briefly present at home (the family's exchange before she leaves). I lean **offstage** — gives the mother space to be the parent on stage and keeps the cast tight.

### Celestial signs of emergence
- The brainstorm establishes physical signatures of emergence (light, sound, displacement). I want to extend this canon: emergences also disturb the **sky**. Pre-emergence, certain stars are not where they should be — or there are more of them than there should be — or one of the wandering stars (a planet, by another name) has moved too far from its track.
- Specific proposal: this village knows a wandering point of light they have a name for — call it **the Walker** (placeholder; you can rename). The Walker has a known path through the seasons. On the night of the emergence, the Walker is in the wrong place. The girl, climbing, sees this — sees that the Walker has gone where it does not go in this season — but she is a child and is not sure, and the not-sureness is part of her fear.
This feels too much on the nose. I think I like just having a constellation here, but maybe the angles are wrong, or now the other stars in the sky (normally not visible) are being enhanced by the onset of the emergence.
- Also: the night sky is "all-too-bright" (your phrase) — too many stars, or stars too sharp, the way a too-clear winter night feels. The reader can take this as either pre-emergence wrongness or just a clear night, the way the chickens-not-settling is both a sign and not.
- This becomes part of the canon for `wiki/concepts/emergence.md` (extending pre-emergence effects to include celestial signs).

### The world-flips / sun-through-earth moment
- v3 had the air "fold." Your edit changed it to "flipped — air solid, earth made of wind." You want a moment where she sees the sun through the (now-transparent) earth. The sun is on the other side of the world; this is night; she briefly sees through the ground to where the sun is. She does not understand it then, and may not later.
- Three versions to consider — I'll draft all three for v4 and you can pick or combine:

  **Version A (transparency):** The earth went thin under her feet. Not gone — she did not fall — but thin, the way ice goes thin on a pond. Through it she saw a light she had no name for, far below, steady, the kind of light you do not look at directly. The slope was over her. The light was under it. Then it was a slope again.

  **Version B (inversion):** The world turned and put its weight where its lightness had been. The air pressed on her like a hand. The ground, beneath her boots, was less than the air. For one breath she stood on nothing and the air held her up, and through the place that should have been ground she saw a light, very far, that had no business being where it was.
  This one is actually my favorite. It leaves more work to the reader to discern the truth of what is happening, but gives a good explanation of what is being seen. Ideally, this page is something that a reader comes back to as they learn more about the world later.

  **Version C (something simpler):** Beneath her feet the slope went out of the world for as long as it takes to forget you are afraid. There was light down there. Bright light. She did not know it was the sun. She would not know it was the sun even later, when she was old enough to know that the sun goes around the other side at night, because what she had seen was not a sun shape, only the brightness of one, and brightness without shape is not a sun.

  My instinct is some hybrid of A and C — A's specificity, C's "she would not know it was the sun even later" — but show all three for you to choose.
