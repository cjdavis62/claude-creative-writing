The tree had folded at the hip like a man who'd taken a blow. She'd walked half a day past the last marking-stone to find it.

Eleven days. She'd been carrying it the whole way.

The figure under the tree raised its head.

She stopped. Her hand was already at the sword — it had been there since the second day, which was about when she'd stopped checking the back trail and let the anger compress into something more useful. She'd used this approach before: the quick close with the blade committed before the target understood what was happening. Most of them were standing.

He was sitting. Both hands in the dirt, open.

She left her hand where it was and stood four paces out. She could hear his breathing from here — thin and careful, the sound of something that had already conceded most of what it was defending and was spending what remained carefully.

That wasn't what she'd come for.

She'd come for something that could absorb eleven days. Something upright, something she could spend herself against. What she found instead was the slump of him against the roots and the sound of his lungs working like a door with a failing hinge.

She knew what gods looked like when they were intact: the cold that arrived in her hands before she registered it arriving. The body deciding before the mind did. None of that. What she felt instead was a wrong note in the backs of her teeth — a frequency that had become mostly silence, a register that was almost gone.

She closed her fists.

"You're late," he said.

She hadn't expected him to speak first.

"I tracked you here," she said.

"I know." He looked at her. Not taking measure — something else, something that cost more. "I've been here three days."

"You waited."

"Yes."

She should have closed the distance. The hesitation was what got people killed. She was standing four paces out with her hand on a sword she hadn't drawn, and he had both hands in the dirt, and she hadn't moved.

"You're dying," she said.

"Yes."

"You knew I was coming to kill you and you waited."

"I knew someone was coming." He paused — the breath costing. "I didn't know it would be—" He stopped. Let it go. When he looked at her again the attention was the same: not measurement, not threat assessment. Recognition. She'd been looked at plenty. This was different.

"What do you want," she said.

He was quiet for long enough that she thought he wasn't going to answer. Then: "You look at what people give their lives to." The breath audible now, each word considered. "And you find the books don't balance."

Her nails were in her palms. She hadn't noticed until she noticed.

"That's not something I chose," she said.

"No."

"Then what use is it to you."

"There's a crossing coming. The last one." He shifted against the root. Pain crossed his face and left. "I need someone there who can see what actually happens — not what the devotees will need to have happened. Someone the truth will land on."

"You don't know me."

"I know that." His eyes on hers. "You still know I'm right."

She had the argument prepared. She'd been building it since somewhere around the fifth day — specific, structured, harder to refuse as the distance closed. She let the silence stand where it would have gone.

The ridge-wind moved across the field. The god watched her. She did not close the distance and did not step back.

"You won't know if I go," she said.

"No."

"You won't know if I don't."

"No." Not an argument. Not a concession. Just the thing as it was. "But you'll know. And there are things you don't leave unfinished."

The argument she'd been carrying was still there. She could feel it. She didn't reach for it.

She turned and walked back across the field.

She was most of the way across when she noticed: the breathing had stopped. She hadn't tracked exactly when. The field behind her was quiet in a way it hadn't been. She didn't look back. Grass came up over her boots and lay back down. At the marking-stone she stopped and opened her hands — small crescents, already browning at the edges. She rubbed her palms on her trousers without thinking about it.

The road was ahead. She'd figure out the direction when she was on it.
