The tree was the kind of tree that outlived its purpose. Bent at the hip by years of ridge-wind, the lower trunk worn pale where sheep had rubbed against it for long enough to leave a mark. She had walked half a day past the last marking-stone to find it.

She had come prepared.

The knife was where she needed it. Eleven days of walking had burned her down to the essential — the fat and the uncertainty gone, only the direction left. She had stopped arguing with herself somewhere around the fifth day. After the fifth day it had become simply: a road, and a thing at the end of it.

The distance between her and the bent shape under the tree was the kind of distance she could close before anyone stopped her.

He was sitting.

She stopped four paces out, her hand already moving, and then her hand stopped. Sitting was wrong. Standing would have given her the shape she had been walking toward for eleven days — something to break against, something that could take the full weight of what she had prepared to spend. What she found instead was the slump of him against the roots. His hands lay open in the dirt. She could hear him breathing from here.

That was not what she had come for.

He raised his head.

His face had the shape of a face that had been in pain for a long time and had organized itself around the management of it. His eyes found her and she felt what she had been told, once, to expect from the gods — but not the way she had been told to expect it. She had been told: *presence*, the quality that made people give themselves over. She felt instead her own back teeth, a resonance in the small bones of her jaw. He had not spoken loudly. He had not, from the look of him, had much to speak from.

The anger she had carried for eleven days had nowhere to go with this.

*He's already—* The thought landed without help from her.

"I know what you came here to do," he said.

"Then you know to be afraid." Her voice came out flat.

"I'm not afraid of you." No dismissal in it. He said it the way a person reports something they have had time to confirm. "I've been waiting three days. I knew your face before you arrived."

"You don't know my face."

"I know your mark." His eyes hadn't left her. The looking was not appraisal. She had been looked at with appraisal enough to know the difference, to know the specific quality of being measured and found. This was different. This was something she had a harder time naming, and the difficulty made her jaw tighten. "Come closer."

"No."

"Then you'll hear this badly. I don't have a voice for distance anymore."

She did not move. He did not press. The silence sat between them, and she understood after a moment that he was not using it — not building pressure, not waiting for her to crack. It was simply the silence of someone with three days behind them and not much in front. She moved forward two paces.

"The mark," she said. "What do you think you know about it."

"I know what it is." He breathed once, collecting. "I know that you can see what people give their lives believing in and find no worth in it. Not because the worth isn't there. Because you've been *shown* what that devotion costs. Not told. Shown."

Her throat did something she did not give it permission to do.

"The imitation gateways." He said it plainly. "The ones built at every holy site — copies of the doors the gods use to cross. The mortals built them out of love. I need you to understand that before anything else. There was no other intention. They saw the gods cross and they honored the crossing, and they made copies, so that more gods might come. It was reverence. The realest thing they had."

"I know what devotion costs," she said. Lower than she meant it.

"Yes." He looked at her. He was still managing the pain and the looking was steady through it. "You know it in your body. Not as a thing you've reasoned to." He breathed again. "The gateways are failing now. The gods are going, and as the last of us go, the structures are breaking. There is one more crossing to come. I need someone to be there when it happens."

"Why me."

"Because everyone who would go to such a place would go looking for a blessing." His voice had dropped, not from weakness but from something that was almost careful — the precision of someone choosing exactly the word. "You would go looking at what is actually there."

The field held them.

She had walked eleven days with one intention and it stood here with her now, unspent, no longer sure of its own shape. The god was looking at her the way she had not been looked at since — since the bond broke, she did not finish the thought. Since the thing she did not let herself think about directly. He was looking at her as if her presence here was accurate. As if the mark on her was not a deprivation but an instrument, and the instrument was the right one for the work.

She hated that it landed. She kept her face still.

"The place," she said.

He told her.

She stood while the information settled. The wind moved through the field grass and touched neither of them.

"When," she said.

He told her that too.

She had seen enough of dying to read the quality of the breathing. She was not going to count on him knowing how it went.

"You won't know if I go."

"No."

"You won't know if I don't."

"No." He shifted slightly against the root and pain crossed his face and left. "But you'll know. And you're not the kind of person who leaves the damage unwalked."

She had the argument ready. She had the specific sentences for it — that being named was not being known, that reading a mark was not reading a life, that she had not agreed to anything and would not be agreeing now.

She was already thinking about the road.

*That's not agreement,* she told herself. *That's the same thing it's been. That's eleven days of walking toward something and not knowing how to stop.*

She looked at him once more. He was still looking at her — steady, undemanding. Not the look of someone who needed a yes. The look of someone who already knew the answer and was giving her the time to arrive at it herself.

She could not remember the last time she had felt, not hoped or believed but *felt*, like something in her was exactly the right shape for something outside her.

She turned and walked back across the field. The grass was longer on the return. The knife was still where she'd put it.

Behind her, he said nothing. She hadn't expected him to.

By the time she reached the marking-stone, the thing he had planted was already past removing.
