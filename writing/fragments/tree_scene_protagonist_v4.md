The tree was the kind of tree that outlived its purpose. Bent at the hip by years of ridge-wind, the lower trunk worn pale where sheep had rubbed against it long enough to leave a mark. She had walked half a day past the last marking-stone to find it.

She had come prepared.

The sword was where she needed it. The eleven days of this road had burned her down to the essential — the fat and the uncertainty gone, only the direction left. She had stopped arguing with herself somewhere around the fifth day. After the fifth day it had become simply: a road, and a thing at the end of it. The goal itself was older than the road. She had stopped trying to trace it back to where it had started and just carried it, the way you carry a debt long enough that it becomes the weight of your own hands.

The figure under the tree was close enough. She had used the opening she was already considering on gods who'd had no warning — the quick close, the sword already committed before they understood what was happening. Most of them had been standing. Most of them had been surprised. She had counted on both.

He was sitting.

She stopped four paces out, her hand already at her sword, and then her hand stopped. Sitting was wrong. She had come prepared to spend everything she'd been carrying on something that could take it — something that would fight back and make the spending mean what it was supposed to mean. What she found instead was the slump of him against the roots. His hands lay open in the dirt. She could hear his breathing from here: thin and deliberate, the breathing of something that had already conceded most of its functions and was defending only the last few.

That was not what she had come for.

He raised his head.

She had killed gods before. She knew what they looked like when they were what they were supposed to be — the weight of a room changing when they entered it, the way attention moved in their direction without asking anyone's permission. None of that was here. His face had learned to carry pain without advertising it. Long-practiced. His eyes found her.

She'd felt divine presence before — the force that moved people toward altars, that made the pious go loose at the knees. What she felt now was different: a resonance at the back of her teeth, in the small bones of her jaw, as though something were using her skull as a channel. He had barely spoken. He did not look, from the state of him, like he had much left to speak from.

She closed her empty fists. Her nails drove into her palms.

"I know what you came here to do," he said.

"Then you know to be afraid."

"No."

He let it sit. The silence had weight — the weight of something long confirmed, no longer in need of defending. Then, after a moment: "I've been here three days. Waiting."

"You don't know me."

"Come closer," he said.

"No."

He spoke then, low, barely carrying the distance. She caught it in her jaw before she caught it in her ears. She took a step forward without deciding to.

"I know what was done to your sight."

She didn't answer for a moment. "Say it then."

"I know what it is to see what people give their lives to and find no worth in it. Not because the worth isn't there. Because you have seen what that devotion costs — in the body, in the years, in what it took from you and didn't give back."

Something pulled tight in her chest.

"There are copies of the divine crossings at every holy site," he said. "The imitation gateways — built by mortals who watched the gods pass and wanted to honor the passage. Hold that first, before anything else: they were built from love. Nothing else behind it. They watched the gods cross, and they honored what they saw, and they built copies, so that more gods might come. It was reverence." He paused. "The truest thing they had."

"I know what devotion costs," she said.

It came out wrong. Her twin surfaced in her mind without permission — the door, the face behind it, the eyes that no longer registered her as real. She pressed her nails deeper.

"Yes." He watched her steadily, the pain managed behind it. "You know it the way the body knows. Not theory. Not distance." He breathed again. "The gateways are failing now. As the gods go, the structures are breaking with us. There is one more crossing left. I need someone to be there when it happens."

"Why me?"

He was quiet for a moment. The ridge-wind moved across the field and was gone.

Then he began to answer — not with what she'd expected, not with what she could receive cleanly. Something that reached past the task and put its hand directly on the wound underneath. She didn't let him finish.

"The place," she said.

He stopped. He looked at her for a moment with something in his face that was not disappointment and not surprise. Then he gave her the place: not as a road-point or a name but as a convergence — a quality of threshold and approach, a thing she would recognize when she was standing in it. She understood it. She held it.

"When," she said.

He told her that too.

She looked at him. She had been around enough dying to read what stage this was. He was not at the beginning of it.

"You won't know if I go."

"No."

"You won't know if I don't."

"No." He shifted slightly against the root and pain crossed his face and left. "But you'll know. And there are things you don't leave unfinished." He said it without plea or pressure — the flat delivery of someone naming a thing they've had a long time to observe. "That's not hope. That's just you."

She had the argument ready. The specific sentences — that being read was not the same as being known, that naming a wound was not understanding a life, that she had not agreed to anything and would not be agreeing now.

She was already thinking about the road.

*That's not agreement,* she told herself. *It's the same thing it's always been. Walking toward something and not finding the place where I stop.*

She turned and walked back across the field.

She was perhaps a third of the way across when the breathing stopped.

She had been tracking it without knowing she was tracking it — the thin, managed sound of it, each breath carefully spent. Now there was nothing. The air behind her was different. Quieter in a way that had nothing to do with sound.

She did not stop. She did not look back.

Her fists were still closed. She opened them as she walked — small crescents of dried blood pressed into both palms, already cooling. She let the field wind cross them.

The marking-stone was ahead. Past it, the road.

Behind her: nothing. Just the field, and the tree, and the silence where the breathing had been.

She kept walking.
