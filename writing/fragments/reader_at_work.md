Forty-one, Tavoen counted. Down from fifty-three. Down from sixty-seven.

The hood sat low and the wrap sat right — pilgrim dress from the outer settlements, bought two towns east where no one knew their face. Three visits to Solaven in three years, three garments, three different entry points into the Tavorael. The priests here were observant. Tavoen was more observant.

The ritual of witness moved at its own pace — slow, older than anyone in the room could remember. One by one, community members came forward to the speaking-stone and said their piece to the god's presence: a name, a loss, a year's work, a child newly born. The god received it. Held it beyond the reach of forgetting. That was the whole of the ritual. That was everything it had ever needed to be.

Except the receiving had changed.

Tavoen watched the woman at the stone. She had spoken her daughter's name — the daughter dead, one year gone, this the first witness-season since. She spoke it clearly. Then she waited.

The waiting had a texture. Had had one, rather. Tavoen had seen this ritual when it worked: the speaker finished, the room shifted in some small way, the speaker felt the shift, stepped back satisfied. The exchange had occurred. The name had left the speaker — received, kept by something that did not die. No single element of the shift could be named — only that it happened, reliably, until the year before last.

The woman waited. The room did not shift. She held the silence a beat longer than she should have needed to, and then stepped back. Her face was still open. She did not know yet that nothing had come back to her. The understanding would arrive in days, maybe weeks — a sense that the year hadn't properly closed, that her daughter's name had come back to her still hers to carry, ungiven to whatever was supposed to keep it. She would think it was her own failure. She would come back next year, say it again, wait again.

Thirty others had waited that way before her. Tavoen was building toward thirty-one of forty-one when the young priest turned.

He was scanning the hall the way the younger ones did — keeping order, not praying. Eyes moving left, right, back. When they reached Tavoen they stopped.

A breath, perhaps. The kind of pause that could be nothing.

Tavoen did not look away. Looking away was the tell. Hold the gaze. Be someone allowed to be here. Be bored. Be devout. Be anything but the thing you are.

The young priest's eyes moved on.

Five more. Five names remaining, and Tavoen needed the full year. Thirty-one of forty-one if they left now — a pattern, but incomplete. Four of sixty-seven the year before last. Thirteen of fifty-three last year. Thirty-one and rising — that was already a progression. The full count would be more than a pattern.

Tavoen stayed.

The young priest made his circuit again. On the second pass he stopped and said something to an older colleague, low. The older priest let his gaze move across the back of the hall without appearing to.

They had learned this here. In this hall. Performing this ritual. Tavoen recognised the technique.

The fourth of five remaining came to the stone — a man presenting his harvest, the amount and quality and land. He waited after. The room did not shift. A moment too long at the stone before he stepped back and made room for the last.

The young priest was a third of the way down the left side of the hall.

The last speaker was a girl of perhaps twelve who spoke so quietly that the hall leaned without meaning to — a grandmother's name offered in barely more than a breath, mouth shaping each syllable with the care of something borrowed and precious. Tavoen read the name more from her lips than from the air.

The young priest was halfway down the left side.

The girl finished. She waited.

The room shifted.

Not much — a small thing, the quality of the air tightening and releasing. But real. The girl felt it. Tavoen saw her feel it, saw her step back with her face smooth and certain in a way almost none of the others had managed today.

The god was not uniformly gone. Something remained, and the something had answered a girl of twelve, not forty adults. Held against three years of numbers, that was not a variation. That was a question.

The young priest reached the back of the hall.

Tavoen turned and walked toward the door. Ordinary pace. Not hurrying. Not looking back. Through the entrance and into the afternoon light of Solaven, where the streets were full of people who did not have the count, who would never know what the waiting had looked like on forty faces.

Thirty-five of forty-one. They had stayed for the last one.

On the street outside the Tavorael, without deciding to, Tavoen counted nine people before the first corner. Then kept walking.
