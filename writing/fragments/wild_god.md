They had heard there was a god in the eastern margins that did not decline.

That was the shape of it, at the start. The cousin of someone's husband had passed through a settlement near the margins and come back with a particular quality of stillness — the kind that belongs to people who have been near something vast and come back. The settlement had stood through three bad winters. Its people had not lost anyone to the fever that had taken thirty from the valley the year before. The god there was old, the cousin had said. Old enough to have become the landscape. You would not see it. You would know it by what it held in place.

It did not decline. That was the sentence. That was the one they kept returning to on the long evenings when the ringing was very bad.

There were nine of them when they set out.

The god had given them something, once. The valley had carried sound differently — a quality of resonance that visitors noticed and could not name, a particular way voices fell in the square. What remained of that gift was the ringing.

The ones born nearest the domain-hall carried the worst of it — three could no longer sleep through the night, and one, a woman who had been born within sight of the hall and had never lived anywhere else, had begun to hear patterns in the noise. Rhythms. The ghost of intervals. The broken music of what the god's domain had been before it turned on itself. She said she would go or she would go mad, and the others believed her.

---

They traveled east for seventeen days.

On the fourth day, one of the older men sat down at the side of the road and did not get up. His eyes were open and he looked at them when they spoke to him, and then looked away. They left him food and water and went on. They did not discuss it. The ringing was bad that morning. There was nothing useful to say.

On the ninth day they lost the road. Not from carelessness — the road simply ended, the way roads do in places where the land has not been organized by human use for long enough. They found a direction and held to it. The tinnitus was worse that stretch, or seemed worse, though perhaps it was only the poor sleep.

On the fourteenth day, the youngest of them fell into a ravine none of them had seen. The sound of it was wrong — not the sound of a fall, but what came after, a quality of silence rising from below that made two of them step back from the edge without speaking. They sat with it for a moment. Then they went on.

There were six of them when the landscape changed.

---

It did not change visibly. The trees were the same trees; the ground was the same ground; the light was where it should have been. But the body's knowledge of itself in space — the animal sureness that says *this way*, *not there*, *you are here* — stopped working reliably. You could see the sun. You could name the directions. But arrival kept coming as a mild surprise, the place you reached not quite the place you had aimed for.

The birds had been getting quieter for two days. On the sixteenth day there were none. The small sounds of the undergrowth — the ordinary busy noise of a forest — had ceased. What remained was wind and footsteps and breathing and the sound that lived inside all of them.

But the wind was wrong. Not absent — present, moving, touching the leaves correctly. She noticed it first as a flatness to the air, a texture missing from the way sound should move through trees. Everything was going somewhere and nothing was returning. The world accepted what was offered and gave nothing back.

No one said this. They walked more carefully, the way you walk when the ground has begun to feel provisional.

---

The tinnitus began to change on the seventeenth day.

At first it worsened — closer, more present, as though whatever produced it had drawn near. The woman born within sight of the hall stopped walking and stood with her eyes closed. The others stopped with her.

Then the ringing stopped.

Not gradually. Between one step and the next, the sound that had lived in all of them — the constant broken music, the god's decline written permanently into their bodies — went away.

The woman opened her eyes.

Two of the others made sounds that were almost laughter. Someone pressed their hands to the sides of their head, unbelieving. The older woman began to cry in a plain, unhurried way, tears moving down her face while she stood still and looked at nothing. They looked at each other and understood: a god was near. Close enough to do this. Close enough to quiet what they had been carrying all this way.

They had been right. The rumor had been right.

She had stopped three steps behind the others.

The silence was wrong.

Not the absence of the ringing — that was gone, and something in her registered it the way it would register the end of any pain. But she could hear her own voice when she tested it, a small word said under her breath, and she could hear it leave and not arrive anywhere. Sound was still going outward — the crying, the footsteps when they moved, the wind — but in one direction only. Toward something. That something was taking it in.

She stood apart. She watched the others moving toward where the sound was going, and knew what they were hearing: an answer. A door. The god receiving them, the silence its welcome, the end of the ringing its first gift.

The sound was moving in one direction and not returning. She understood something else.

---

She did not know how long she stood there. Long enough for the others to become shapes between the trees. Long enough for the shapes to become suggestions, and the suggestions to become simply the trees.

She called out once. The sound left.

Then she turned and walked back the way they had come.

The sounds returned slowly. Her footsteps first — the particular scrape of her feet on the path, landing and staying landed. Then wind returning from a tree to her left. Then a single bird, distant, stopping, starting again. She tracked each one. She was counting something, though she could not have said what.

The tinnitus came back two days from the road.

Faintly at first — a thread of sound, the broken interval she had lived with for so long she had forgotten what its absence felt like. She stopped walking. Stood in the middle of the path while it gathered itself, thin and then less thin, the familiar terrible music of a dying god still reaching, still transmitting, still losing itself.

She stood there for a long time.

Then she kept walking.

It was raining by the time she reached the road. Her footsteps on the wet stone were loud in a way that felt deliberate — sound landing, sound returning, sound that came back when she made it.
