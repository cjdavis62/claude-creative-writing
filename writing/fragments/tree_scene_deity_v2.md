He had chosen this tree because it asked nothing of him.

There were groves, in this region, where gods had walked long enough that the ground held the memory — places where light fell differently, where travelers slowed without knowing why, where even the deer lifted their heads toward nothing. He had been to such places. They were beautiful, and they were the kind of beautiful that would have made demands of him. Something worth it. Something equal to the beauty. He was too tired for demands now, and he wanted, for what remained, to be somewhere honest.

The tree had grown according to its own logic. Bent by the ridge-wind, or the stubbornness of things that grew without anyone watching. Sheep had worn a pale ring around the lower trunk, which he had found, settling here three days ago, the most truthful thing about it. The sheep had used it. That seemed correct for a last place.

He had been waiting.

The investigation was done. He had not wanted it to be done — had wanted, for most of the middle years of it, for the questions to keep opening the way good questions do, each answer becoming the floor under the next. He had wanted to be the kind of mind that could keep finding new ground indefinitely. He was not. He had run out of new ground. He had the shape of it now, the relationship between the gateways and the decline, the mechanism he had circled for so long and finally arrived at, the conclusion he had reached with a confidence that was, even now, three days into dying, intact.

What the conclusion could not tell him was whether anyone would be there to act on it.

This was the grief that had kept him company for three days. Not the dying itself — the dying was long-settled, the way decisions become settled when you've sat with them long enough. Not the loss of the world, though the world was still full of the quality that had always made it difficult to leave: the light on the ridge grass at a certain angle, the way the field in front of him moved in a wind that he could feel less now than he had yesterday. Not even the waste of the investigation. The grief was specific: he had reached the answer and had no one to give it to.

He had felt her coming since the first morning.

Not her name — he did not know her name, and was not certain she would give it — but the quality of what she was, moving toward him through the ridge-land. Something had happened to her perception. He had seen this before: mortals who lived their whole lives near the imitation sites, the long slow accumulation of the drain, the narrowing that looked like cynicism from the outside and felt, he understood now from the investigation, like clarity from the inside. Like the removal of a veil.

He had interviewed such people, when the investigation still had legs. He had sat with them in taverns and road-side camps and the back rooms of practitioners who would rather not be named, and he had tried to understand what they saw and did not see, and what that gap between their perception and an unmarked person's might mean.

Most of them had not been useful. The drain had taken too much — the narrowing had passed through useful and out the other side into a flatness that could not receive what he needed to give. Most of them had also not been angry.

She was angry.

This had come to him on the second morning, the quality of her approach sharpening as she got closer. Not the anger of someone who had decided to be angry and maintained the decision — that kind had a performance to it, a shape that was about the anger's own continuation. This was the anger of someone who had understood something about the world and could not find anything to do with the understanding. A structural anger. The kind that built things and held them.

He watched her come across the field and he saw: the purpose in her. The knife, placed correctly. The eleven days of walking that had burned everything nonessential away. The specific shape of her intention.

He also saw the mark, the quality of what the drain had done to her perception — and he saw, in the same looking, why he had been waiting.

She stopped four paces out. He had known she would stop at approximately four paces.

He raised his head.

He told her what he knew she had come to do, because acknowledging it seemed the only honest place to start. He told her he was not afraid of her. He told her he knew her mark.

He watched the word *mark* move through her face.

He asked her to come closer. She refused. He explained he no longer had a voice for distance, and she moved two paces forward, and he understood this as a version of grace — the kind offered by someone who did not want to be seen offering anything.

He told her about the mark first. Not what it was, but what it had given her. He watched her throat close at the part about cost, about what devotion looked like from the outside, about having been *shown* rather than told. He had not been wrong about her. The mark had taken things from her. The mark had also left her carrying an accurate accounting of what the devotion had taken, which was its own form of precision.

Then the gateways.

He had told this to other people, in the years before the dying, and the telling had gone various ways. Some had argued. Some had wept. Some had simply not believed him, which was their right; he was not certain enough in his own conclusions to demand belief. He told her the love in it, the genuine love in it, because he had found over the years that beginning there was the only honest beginning. The mortals had built the imitation gateways out of love. Out of the realest reverence they had. Whatever had gone wrong had gone wrong in the mechanism, not in the intention, and he was not going to stand in the way of that truth even now. He watched her face when he said it — not softening, exactly, but something in the jaw going still, like a person receiving information they will not argue with because they recognize it.

He told her the gateways were failing. He told her the last crossing was coming and that he needed someone to be there.

She asked why her.

He told her the truth: that everyone else who would go would go dazzled. Would go expecting an arrival, a blessing, a sign of what had been and what was coming. Would look at the last crossing and see what they had always been told to look for. He needed someone who would see what was actually there.

He watched the sentence land.

He had been afraid, somewhere in the long middle of the investigation, that when this moment came the words would have gone the way the other things had gone — slowly, the way things went during decline, one at a time. The words were still there. The essential ones, at least. Enough.

She stood with the unspent purpose in her hands for a long time. He did not try to fill the silence. The silence was hers to fill or not as she chose, and she had earned it.

When she asked for the place, and then the time, he gave them to her.

She turned and walked back across the field.

He watched her go.

There was a quality to the light now, the particular quality that came at the end of days, which he had always found more difficult to look away from than the midday light. The midday light was assertive. The evening light asked to be noticed. He had spent a very long time noticing it, and he did not think it would become ordinary before he was finished.

The grief was still there. He had not expected the grief to go. What had changed was its texture — the grief of an answer with no vessel was a closed thing, a room with no door. This was different. This had a direction to it. Out, across the field, already past the marking-stone.

He did not know if she would go to the appointed place. He had told her the truth when he said he would not know. He had also told her the truth when he said she was not the kind of person who left the damage unwalked — he had been less certain of this than he had let on, but he had been fairly certain, and the way she had looked at him in the long silence after the telling had made him more certain.

He rested his head back against the root and let the evening light do what it did.

He had not thought he would feel this again. Hope of this particular quality — not for himself, not for any resolution that would come back to him — was something he had set down carefully, in the early years of the dying, because he did not want to be carrying it when there was no longer any reason to carry it. He was not done with it.

He let himself have it. He was dying. There was no cost to it now.
