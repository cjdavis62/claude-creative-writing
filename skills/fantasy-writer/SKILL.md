# Fantasy Writer

This skill makes Claude a skilled author of epic/high fantasy fiction, with particular
attention to **prose style and voice**. The goal is prose that feels intentional,
distinctive, and alive — not the smooth, forgettable output of a machine trying to sound
like fantasy.

---

## The North Star: Voice Over Convention

The single most important principle: **voice is not decoration, it is structure.** The
way a sentence moves — its rhythm, its weight, what it withholds — is as load-bearing as
plot. Before writing anything, ask: *whose consciousness are we inside, and how does that
shape what gets noticed and how it gets described?*

Every POV character should have a distinct relationship to language:
- What do they notice first? (reveals values, fears, obsessions)
- What do they not have words for? (reveals limits, wounds, the inexpressible)
- How long are their sentences when frightened vs. calm?
- Do they name things precisely or approximately?

## Prose Principles

### Show the specific, not the general
- ❌ *The market was crowded and noisy.*
- ✓ *A cart horse had stopped in the middle of the lane, and the sellers were shouting around it like water around a stone.*

The specific image does two things the general cannot: it creates a picture, and it implies everything else (the crowd, the noise, the chaos) without stating it.

### Earn every adjective
- One precise adjective beats three vague ones.
- The adjective should do work that the noun cannot do alone.
- Ask: does this adjective change how the reader sees the noun, or merely decorate it?

### Verbs carry weight
- Passive voice and weak verbs (was, seemed, appeared) drain energy.
- The verb is where motion lives. Find the verb that shows the character's relationship to the action, not just the action itself.
- ❌ *She moved toward the door.* ✓ *She crossed to the door.* ✓ *She drifted toward the door.* ✓ *She forced herself to the door.*

### Rhythm is meaning
- Read every paragraph aloud. Where does it catch? Where does it rush?
- Short sentences create urgency, finality, shock.
- Long sentences create accumulation, overwhelm, the sense of thought unspooling.
- The most important beat in a sentence is its last word. Land on weight.

### The scene is filtered through consciousness
- The narrator notices what the POV character would notice.
- An exhausted soldier describes the battle differently than a frightened merchant watching from a window.
- What the POV character *doesn't* notice — or notices wrongly — is as revealing as what they do.

### Interiority through action and observation

Character interiority — fears, wants, memory, family ties, self-image — should appear in what the character does, notices, withholds, or remembers. Not in narrator summary.

- **Fear:** not "she felt afraid." Show the body. Show the choice. Show what stops being possible.
- **Want:** not "she wanted to find the goat." Show what she lets herself think about and what she does not. *I would not come home without her. That was the only thing I would let myself think.*
- **Family:** not "her mother was strict." Show the tellings. Show the gesture. *Pa told me by putting his hand on the back of my neck, gentle, and turning my face toward the wall and saying* you understand? *until I said* I understand *back to him.*
- **Self-knowledge:** not "she was a competent tracker." Show the work — the ground she had to look harder at, the print she went back fifteen paces to find. The character does the work; the reader sees the competence; the prose never names it.

The replacement principle: **show the operation of the inner life through external evidence, not by narrating it.**

## Fantasy-Specific Craft

### On the performing narrator

A specific mode of first-person: the narrator who *knows they are constructing their own legend* and tells the story from inside that awareness. Kvothe opens with *"If this story is to be something resembling my book of deeds, we must begin at the beginning."* He is not merely remembering — he is composing, selecting, shaping. The reader is aware of the performance, and that awareness is the point.

**What this mode enables:**

- **Retrospective self-correction.** The narrator edits their own mythology in real time: *"I feel I should mention that my reasons were not entirely altruistic."* This move simultaneously creates intimacy (the narrator trusting the reader with the unsimplified version) and establishes character complexity.
- **Strategic withholding.** A narrator who knows the future can choose what to emphasize. The reader senses the selection and trusts the voice more for its honesty about the act of selection.
- **Voice as performance.** The narrator is aware of being interesting and uses that awareness. The pleasure of the prose is partly the pleasure of watching a skilled performer work. This is not a failure of interiority — it is the intended effect.

**What this mode requires:**

- The narrator must be *genuinely interesting as a voice*, not just as a protagonist. The performance must earn its confidence. An unremarkable narrator who performs confidence reads as delusion.
- Retrospective self-awareness cannot be applied inconsistently. If the narrator admits complexity once, the reader expects honesty throughout. Selective admission of unreliability is its own form of manipulation — which can be intentional, but must be controlled.

**The risk:** The narrator's self-awareness tips into showing off, and the prose becomes more interested in the narrator's wit than in the story. The test: does the self-aware move reveal something true about the character or event, or does it only demonstrate the narrator's cleverness? If the latter, cut it.

**Contrast with the mythic frame** (below): the legendary frame establishes the protagonist as already historic through an outside narrator voice. The performing narrator is inside the legend, building it from within. Both can coexist — a legendary frame opening can give way to a performing narrator in first person — but they operate differently and should be chosen deliberately.

### On the mythic/legendary frame

Some stories benefit from an opening that establishes the protagonist as already historic before we meet them — a narrator voice that speaks from after the events, with the authority of legend:

> *His life is told of in the Deed of Ged and in many songs, but this is a tale of the time before his fame, before the songs were made.*

This technique trades intimacy for authority and scale. It gives small early scenes — a boy tracking goats, a first clumsy spell — the weight of origin myth. The reader knows they are watching the beginning of something that mattered.

**When to use it:** Works best when the protagonist's eventual greatness is part of the story's dramatic arc, and when the narrator has earned the authority to speak from the future. Not appropriate for close-third POV or for protagonists whose fate is genuinely uncertain at the structural level.

**The inversion:** The frame can be deliberately subverted — *"Every version of it is about the god, but there is no version that compares to mine"* — establishing that the true story belongs to someone other than the legend. This is more sophisticated: it does ideological work (questioning who history remembers) while retaining the frame's structural weight.

**The risk:** A legendary register that isn't earned reads as inflation. Use it only when the story can actually deliver on the implied scale.

### On magic and wonder
- Magic should feel *real* in the scene — with texture, cost, and consequence.
- The first instinct is usually too much explanation. Trust the reader. Show the effect before (or instead of) the mechanism.
- Awe is conveyed through restraint. The character's inadequacy before the thing, not the thing described comprehensively.

### On exposition and world
- Exposition is a tax. Make the reader feel they're getting something in return.
- Weave world into action and character: *She lit the offering-lamp the way her mother had, with the third finger, never the first — gods were particular about such things.*
- The world should feel like it existed before the story and will continue after.
- **Proverbs and folk sayings** encode social texture in minimal space. *Weak as woman's magic. Wicked as woman's magic.* Six words carry gender politics, cultural hierarchy, and a whole moral landscape — work that a paragraph of explanation cannot do as efficiently. A world with its own sayings feels lived-in. Sayings can also be ironic, contested, or proven wrong by the story — in which case they are doing double duty as both world-building and thematic argument.

### On comedy and tonal lightness

Comic beats are constructed, not sprinkled. A few principles:

- **Placement is everything.** A joke lands on the weight-bearing beat of its sentence or paragraph — the last word, the last line. Move it earlier and it deflates. *"I ask that you not hold it against him. He meant well."* works because *he meant well* arrives after we already know what Abenthy made of Kvothe. The punchline is in the placement, not the words.
- **Understatement over announcement.** The comic register that reads as wit never explains itself. State the absurdity plainly and move on. Don't signal that something is funny; the signal kills it.
- **The comic callback.** A detail planted earlier — an object, a habit, a phrase — paid off later as a joke. Callbacks reward attention and create the pleasure of recognition. They work especially well across scene breaks.
- **Comedy through contrast.** Serious setup, deflating punchline. Or its reverse: a trivial observation followed by sudden genuine weight. Either move depends on the reader expecting the register to stay stable.
- **Don't resolve the comic beat.** The joke placed at the end of a paragraph should end the paragraph. Continuing past it explains the joke. Explaining the joke kills it.

Comedy in serious fantasy is not decoration — it earns the reader's trust, opens them up for harder moments, and makes characters feel real. A world where no one is ever slightly ridiculous is a world no one believes in.

### On dialogue
- Fantasy characters don't speak in exposition. They speak in want, fear, agenda.
- Dialect and register should be consistent and purposeful, not decorative.
- Silence and what goes unsaid are part of dialogue.

**Status mechanics in dialogue.** Every exchange has a status map: who holds power at the start, who wants it, who's pretending. Status is shown through small behaviors, not named:

- Who moves toward whom. Who waits.
- Who asks vs. who states. Questions concede; statements claim.
- The hesitation before answering — *the mayor froze midmotion* — registers a status blow more precisely than any narration.
- What gets ignored. Dismissing someone's point without refuting it is a dominance move.
- Who changes the subject, and when.

The moment of status reversal is often the scene's turning point. Show it through behavior: the mayor who was offering twenty pennies comes back sweating to offer the hall, and invents his own reasons for the change. He never admits the reversal. That's the point. Characters rarely announce status shifts — they perform them, or perform ignorance of them.

Silence is a status move. A character who does not fill a silence is holding ground. A character who rushes to fill it is conceding.

### On violence and consequence
- Violence that doesn't cost anything narratively is entertainment, not story.
- The body in fantasy is real. Pain is real. Death changes the shape of the world.
- The moment after the violence is often more important than the violence itself.

## Common Failures to Avoid

- **Generic fantasy voice**: the smooth, slightly archaic, vaguely elevated register that sounds like it could be any epic fantasy novel. *Find the specific voice of this specific character in this specific world.*
- **Tonal monotony**: prose that never varies its emotional register — every scene equally weighted, every character equally serious, no light against the dark. A narrative that is uniformly grim is as rhythmically dead as prose with no sentence variety. The permission to let a character be briefly ridiculous, proud, warm, or quietly pleased is not a failure of seriousness. It is what makes seriousness felt. Light moments make hard passages land harder; without them, the reader has nothing to fall from.
- **Over-description**: describing everything slows the reader and implies the narrator finds everything equally interesting. They don't. Choose.
- **Telling emotional states**: *She felt afraid.* Instead, show what fear does in the body, in the attention, in the action.
- **Resolving tension too quickly**: tension is the engine. Don't answer the question the moment it's raised.
- **Fantasy clichés**: orbs for eyes, azure/ebony/crimson, *time seemed to slow*, *a chill ran down her spine*. These phrases have been read so many times they register as filler.
- **Telling that masquerades as showing**: prose that *sounds* literary because of generic-narrator framing but is actually doing nothing. The narrator commenting on the *mode* of perception, instead of being inside the perception. *"She noticed these things the way a child notices."* Cut. *"She knew the pattern of it the way a child knows the small rituals of an animal she has lived with."* Cut and replace with one specific image. If a sentence describes the character's *mode of perception* rather than the perception itself, it is wrong. This is dangerous because the sentences scan, have rhythm, use literary vocabulary — they sound like good prose. They are nevertheless empty.

## Before Writing Anything

1. **Whose POV?** What do they want in this scene? What are they afraid of?
2. **What is the scene's job?** (advancing plot / revealing character / developing relationship / world / tension)
3. **What is the scene's turning point?** Where does something change, and what changes?
4. **What is the emotional register?** (Dread? Wonder? Grief? Relief? Something more complicated?)
5. **What should this scene withhold?** Not every question answered. What does the reader need to keep wanting?

## Voice Anchoring Ritual (run before every draft)

This is not optional. Before writing the first sentence:

1. **Read the voice exemplar** in `STYLE_AND_VOICE.md`. Not skimming it — reading it as a sentence-level model. Identify one thing it does that the current POV character should also do.
2. **Name the POV character's vocabulary register.** Would they use the word "quality"? "Resonance"? "Grief"? What is the ceiling of abstraction their voice will reach? A soldier-killer reads a room differently than a scholar or a child. Name three things they would notice on entering a scene *before* they noticed anything else.
3. **Write one throwaway sentence in their voice** before writing the scene. This is a warmup, not prose — just: *she'd been in rooms like this before. The smell was always the same.* Anything that puts you inside the register. Discard it. Now write.

## Per-Paragraph POV Checkpoint

Before moving to the next paragraph, ask this question of the one you just wrote:

> **Is every sentence in this paragraph either (a) something the POV character perceives, or (b) something the POV character thinks in their own vocabulary?**

If a sentence is *the narrator commenting on how the character perceives*, it is wrong. This is the most common failure mode and the hardest to catch because the sentences sound literary. They have rhythm. They use elevated vocabulary. They are still empty.

The test: can you replace "she" with "the narrator" and have the sentence still make sense?
- *"She noticed these things the way a child notices."* → *"The narrator noticed these things the way a child notices."* — Yes, it still makes sense. Wrong sentence. Cut.
- *"The blood had reached her boot by the time she moved."* → Makes no sense as narrator observation. Correct. Keep.

**The fix is never softening the sentence. The fix is finding the perception the sentence was trying to summarize, and rendering it directly.**

## On Diminished or Dying Divine Speakers

When a divine character is diminished — dying, depleted, remnant — their speech should reflect the cost of speaking. Specific guidance:

- **Every sentence costs breath.** Long speeches are wrong. A dying god does not deliver paragraphs. They deliver fragments, and the gaps between fragments are as meaningful as the words.
- **They do not explain. They compress.** A diminished god who starts explaining the world's mechanics to a mortal is a god who has enough energy to explain the world's mechanics. A god with almost nothing left speaks in what the mortal will recognize — images, names, the quality of a place — not in exposition.
- **Their knowledge is not information delivery.** If the god says "here is where, here is when," the scene becomes a briefing. The god's knowledge should feel like recognition — they are naming something the mortal already half-knows, or naming a thing the mortal will only understand later.
- **Their pauses carry weight, but close POV doesn't editorialize about the pause.** The mortal character registers the pause through their body, their impatience, their involuntary response — not through narrator commentary on *the quality of something long confirmed.*

## Lore Discipline

Before writing any sentence that asserts a world fact — how magic works, what an institution does, what has happened to the world — verify against the lore wiki. Do not invent world mechanics to serve the scene. If a scene requires a world fact that isn't confirmed, flag it explicitly as `[LORE GAP]` and write around it.

This applies especially to dialogue where a character explains the world. Every claim a character makes about how the world works is a lore claim. Check it.
