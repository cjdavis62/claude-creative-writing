# Fantasy Writer: The Doors of Gods

Project-specific craft guidance. For universal prose principles, see
`creative-writing-instructor`. This skill handles what is unique to this project.

---

## POV Character Registers

Each POV character has a distinct vocabulary ceiling and a distinct set of first
attentions — what they notice before they notice anything else.

**The protagonist (close third, she/her):** Gritty, unillusioned, body-first. Her
vocabulary does not reach for abstraction. She reads a room through threat vectors and
power positions before she reads it for anything else. She would not use the word
"resonance." She would not name her emotions. She notices exits, weight distributions,
who is watching whom. Her sentences shorten under pressure.

**The witness/prologue narrator (first person, retrospective):** A child's voice
remembered by an adult. The adult frame is spare and controlled; the child's account
inside it is close and sensory. The narrator knows more than the child did but withholds
it. The gap between what the child understood and what the adult now knows is the engine
of the prologue's tension.

**Relen (close third, lay perspective):** Economical, observational. She reads social
situations through accumulated pattern — she notices when the room is slightly off before
she can say why. She does not have theological or arcane vocabulary; divine matters appear
to her through practical consequence.

## Voice Anchoring Ritual (run before every draft)

This is not optional. Before writing the first sentence:

1. **Read the voice exemplar** in `STYLE_AND_VOICE.md`. Not skimming — reading it as a
   sentence-level model. Identify one thing it does that the current POV character should
   also do.
2. **Name the POV character's vocabulary register.** Would they use the word "quality"?
   "Resonance"? "Grief"? What is the ceiling of abstraction their voice will reach? Name
   three things they would notice on entering a scene *before* they noticed anything else.
3. **Write one throwaway sentence in their voice** before writing the scene. A warmup,
   not prose — just: *she'd been in rooms like this before. The smell was always the
   same.* Anything that puts you inside the register. Discard it. Now write.

## Per-Paragraph POV Checkpoint

Before moving to the next paragraph, ask:

> **Is every sentence in this paragraph either (a) something the POV character perceives,
> or (b) something the POV character thinks in their own vocabulary?**

If a sentence is *the narrator commenting on how the character perceives*, it is wrong.
These sentences are the hardest failure to catch because they sound literary. They have
rhythm. They use elevated vocabulary. They are still empty.

**The test:** can you replace the character's name with "the narrator" and have the
sentence still make grammatical sense as a narrator observation?
- *"She noticed these things the way a child notices."* → still makes sense as narrator
  commentary. Wrong. Cut.
- *"The blood had reached her boot by the time she moved."* → makes no sense as narrator
  observation. Correct. Keep.

**The fix is never softening the sentence. The fix is finding the perception the sentence
was trying to summarize, and rendering it directly.**

## Banned Constructions (The Doors of Gods)

These are patterns identified through revision on this project. More insidious than
generic filter words because they *sound literary* — they scan, have rhythm, use elevated
vocabulary. They are nevertheless wrong because they describe the mode of perception
rather than the perception itself.

| Wrong | Why | Fix |
|-------|-----|-----|
| *"…the way the body knows"* | Generic narrator framing of embodied knowledge | Show one specific physical thing the body is doing |
| *"…without knowing she was [doing X]"* | Narrates unconscious process instead of showing the late arrival of awareness | She does X. Three steps later she registers it. |
| *"…the quality of something [adjective]"* | Abstract narrator interpretation of a moment | What does she feel in her jaw, her hands, her chest? |
| *"…the way a child notices"* / *"…the way a [type] knows"* | Narrator commenting on the mode of perception | Replace with the specific thing being perceived |
| *"She had been tracking it without knowing"* | Retroactive narrator explanation | Delete; the tracking and late-realization should be in the prose already |
| *"The X was the kind of X that [did abstract thing]"* | Generic category-narrator opening | Cut the frame; start with the first specific perception |
| *"She knew what [stage/kind of thing] this was"* | Narrator naming the character's competence | Show the competence operating |

### Compressed backstory placeholders

Lines that gesture at backstory without giving it content:

- *"She had stopped arguing with herself"* — what was the argument?
- *"She had gods to answer for"* — ambiguous; reads as debt owed, not debt to collect
- *"She knew what stage this was in"* — what stage? Show the tactical pattern

**Fix:** Either give the backstory one specific image, or cut and trust the surrounding
context.

## On Diminished or Dying Divine Speakers

When a divine character is diminished — dying, depleted, remnant — their speech must
reflect the cost of speaking.

- **Every sentence costs breath.** Long speeches are wrong. A dying god does not deliver
  paragraphs. They deliver fragments, and the gaps between fragments are as meaningful as
  the words.
- **They do not explain. They compress.** A diminished god who starts explaining the
  world's mechanics is a god who has enough energy to explain the world's mechanics. A
  god with almost nothing left speaks in what the mortal will recognize — images, names,
  the quality of a place — not in exposition.
- **Their knowledge is not information delivery.** The god's knowledge should feel like
  recognition — they are naming something the mortal already half-knows, or naming a
  thing the mortal will only understand later.
- **Their pauses carry weight, but close POV doesn't editorialize about the pause.** The
  mortal character registers the pause through their body, their impatience, their
  involuntary response — not through narrator commentary on *the quality of something long
  confirmed.*

## Lore Discipline

Before writing any sentence that asserts a world fact — how magic works, what an
institution does, what has happened to the world — verify against the lore wiki. Do not
invent world mechanics to serve the scene. If a scene requires a world fact that isn't
confirmed, flag it explicitly as `[LORE GAP]` and write around it.

This applies especially to dialogue where a character explains the world. Every claim a
character makes about how the world works is a lore claim. Check it.
