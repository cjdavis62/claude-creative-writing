# Worldbuilding

This skill provides a structured framework for creating fantasy worlds that feel
internally consistent, historically plausible, and narratively generative. The goal
is not comprehensiveness but *coherence* — a world where every element implies and
supports every other.

---

## The Core Principle: Consequence Chains

Every world element should have:
1. **A cause** — why does this exist? What historical, geographical, or metaphysical
   force produced it?
2. **Consequences** — what does this change about daily life, power structures,
   belief systems?
3. **Tensions** — what conflicts does this element create or sustain?

A magic system that costs the user memories doesn't just affect mages — it shapes
how mages are regarded, whether power accrues to the young or old, whether wealthy
families buy magic at the cost of their children's pasts. Pull one thread; the whole
cloth moves.

## Domain Frameworks

### Magic Systems

**First questions:**
- Is magic *learnable* or *innate*? (Shapes class and power structures)
- What is the *source*? (Divine, environmental, internal, sacrificial)
- What is the *cost*? (Physical, temporal, moral, relational, existential)
- Who has *access*? (Rare/common; restricted/free)
- Is it *knowable*? (Hard rules vs. soft mystery — each has different narrative uses)

**Sanderson's Laws (internalized, not dogmatic):**
1. A magic system's ability to solve problems is proportional to how well the reader
   understands it. Mystery is fine; *convenient* mystery is not.
2. Limitations and costs are more interesting than powers.
3. Expand existing magic before adding new magic.

**For epic/high fantasy:** Soft magic (mysterious, awe-inspiring, unpredictable) serves
wonder and horror. Hard magic (systematic, learnable) serves plot and character agency.
Most great fantasy worlds have both in tension.

### Geography & Ecology

Geography *determines* everything else: trade routes, military strategy, cultural
contact, religious sites, resource conflicts.

**Build order:**
1. Major geographical features first (mountain ranges, rivers, coastlines, deserts)
2. Settlement patterns emerge from geography (cities at river confluences, passes,
   natural harbors)
3. Trade routes connect settlements; culture follows trade
4. Political borders follow geography where possible; where they don't, there's
   always a war or an old treaty

**Questions that generate story:**
- What resources are scarce? Who controls them?
- What natural barrier has never been crossed, and why?
- Where do cultures collide, and what grew in that collision?

---

### Political Structures

Power is always about who controls what: violence, resources, legitimacy, information.

**For each political entity, establish:**
- *Basis of legitimacy* — why do people accept this authority? (Divine right,
  conquest, election, tradition, competence)
- *Mechanisms of succession* — how does power transfer? (This is where drama lives)
- *Internal factions* — no monolithic empires; every court has a left and right hand
- *External pressures* — who threatens them, who do they threaten?
- *What they want that they don't have*

**Avoiding the monolithic empire:** Even "the evil empire" should have reformers,
pragmatists, true believers, opportunists, and people just trying to survive. Complexity
is not sympathy — it's texture.

---

### Cultures & Peoples

Culture is the accumulated response to environment, history, and neighbor-pressure.

**For each culture:**
- *What do they value most, and why?* (Trace it to a historical event or geography)
- *What are their founding myths?* (These reveal what they need to believe about
  themselves)
- *How do they mark transitions?* (Birth, adulthood, marriage, death — ritual is
  character)
- *What do they consider shameful?* (Often more revealing than what they admire)
- *How do they talk about outsiders?*

**On language and naming:**
- Consistent phonetic rules per culture (hard stops vs. flowing vowels vs. consonant
  clusters) do more than any naming convention
- Idioms, oaths, and profanity are worldbuilding shortcuts — "by the Pale" implies
  a whole cosmology
- Avoid: mixing real-world cultural aesthetics superficially (the "vaguely Japanese"
  empire)

---

### Religion & Cosmology

In worlds where gods exist or existed, religion is not metaphor — it is politics,
psychology, and physics simultaneously.

**Key questions:**
- Are gods *real and present*, *real but absent*, or *unknown*?
- What do they want from mortals?
- What happens when they die? (As in: what is the world-consequence, not just the
  theological one)
- What is the relationship between divine power and mortal faith — does one sustain
  the other?
- What heresies exist, and who benefits from them?

**For dying/dead god cosmologies specifically:**
- What filled the power vacuum?
- Is the world better or worse without them?
- Do mortals still pray, and if so, to what?
- What relics, rituals, or bloodlines persist?

---

### History & Time

History is not backstory — it is *pressure*. The past presses on every scene.

**Layering history:**
1. Ancient/mythic era — only partially known, more legend than fact
2. Historical era — written records, contested interpretation
3. Living memory — events characters or their parents survived
4. Present — where the story takes place

Every character has a relationship to all four layers. A peasant's relationship to
the ancient era is superstition and folk tale. A scholar's is contested academic
record. A god's is direct memory — and possibly guilt.

## Worldbuilding Anti-Patterns

- **The setting that exists to be cool, not to be inhabited.** Every visual spectacle
  should have a reason it exists and people who depend on it.
- **The monoculture continent.** A landmass the size of Europe with one culture,
  one religion, one language.
- **Magic that only affects plot.** Magic should be woven into economics, medicine,
  warfare, religion, daily life — or its absence should be explained.
- **History that only goes back to the inciting event.** Characters should feel like
  they live in the *middle* of history, not at its beginning.
- **The chosen one who changes everything.** The world should feel like it would have
  kept turning without the protagonist.

## Wiki Integration

After any worldbuilding session, create or update wiki entries for all elements
established. Use the `lore-wiki` skill to:
- Create entries for new locations, factions, magic systems
- Update cosmology entries with new divine facts
- Add timeline entries for historical events discussed
- Flag any new elements that create tensions with existing entries
