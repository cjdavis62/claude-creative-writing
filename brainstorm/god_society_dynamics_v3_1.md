## I. The Emergence Event

> **Status: LARGELY RESOLVED.** The emergence mechanic, physical signature, pre-emergence effects (animals, celestial signs, thresholds-of-life sensitivity), and the visible gateway/door are all canon-committed in `wiki/concepts/emergence.md` and `DECISIONS.md` (multiple entries 2026-05-01). ### The Flashpoint

A god's emergence is sudden and locationally specific. It is not announced. It does not follow a schedule. One moment the membrane between planes is intact; the next, it is not, and a god is standing somewhere in the mortal world — in a field, on a riverbank, in the middle of a market, at the edge of a cliff, inside someone's home.

The emergence itself has a physical signature — light, sound, a displacement of air, a sensation in the body of anyone nearby. But the nature of that signature varies by god and is not standardized. Two people present at the same emergence may describe it entirely differently. This becomes important later: the emergence account is the founding document of a society's theology, and it is already unreliable at the moment of its creation.

What follows the emergence immediately is a period of profound disorientation — for the god and for any witnesses. The god has just crossed between planes. They have lost something in the crossing; they do not yet know what. They may not know where they are, what language is being spoken, whether it is safe. The witnesses do not know what they are looking at — only that something has happened that has no precedent in their experience.

This early window — before anyone has decided what the emergence *means* — is the most theologically unguarded moment in a society's history. What happens in it, and who is present, shapes everything that follows.

### Who Witnesses an Emergence

The accident of witness is one of the most consequential forces in this world's history. A god who emerges in front of a single shepherd will have a completely different early relationship with the mortal world than one who emerges in front of a crowded marketplace. A god witnessed first by a child, or a priest, or a soldier, or a merchant, or an outcast — each of these produces a different founding narrative, a different set of first interpretations, a different claim to authority.

Societies tend to mythologize the first witnesses heavily. The shepherd who saw the god becomes the ancestor of a priestly class. The child who was present becomes a symbol of innocence and divine favor. In some cultures, the first witness is considered permanently marked — blessed, or burdened, or both. In others, the identity of the first witness is disputed and the dispute never fully resolved.

**A recurring dynamic:** the person who actually witnessed the emergence first is not always the person who ends up with the authority that derives from it. Proximity to power attracts revision.

### The God's Condition at Emergence

Gods arrive incomplete. This is now understood as simply the cost of crossing — memory and power are lost in transit. But the *degree* of loss varies, and the pattern of that variation is one of the things the dying deity's investigation is trying to map.

What is consistently lost:
- Specific memories from the divine plane — names, events, relationships, the texture of what it was like to be there
- Some portion of their power, which must rebuild over time
- Their sense of orientation — early gods are often described as confused, searching, reaching for something they can no longer find

What is inconsistently lost:
- Language (some arrive speaking mortal tongues fluently; others arrive in silence and must learn)
- A sense of self — most retain who they *are*, but some arrive with a fragmented or uncertain identity
- Their domain — most gods have a sphere of power or concern that persists through the crossing, but in some it seems muted or changed

**The emerging pattern the dying deity has noticed but not yet explained:** gods who emerge near sites where imitation gateways have been built in advance lose more. This is attributed, in most theological traditions, to the sanctity of the site drawing more divine presence through — a greater opening. The opposite is true, but the misreading is understandable.

**What gods feel in the moment of crossing:** gods do experience a sensation at the moment of emergence — a wrongness, a pull, something draining away. Most interpret this as the known cost of transition and do not examine it further. For the dying deity, witnessing an emergence from close proximity will be the moment that first suggests the mechanism is not what he thought: the wrongness is directional. Something is being drawn *back*. This is the crack in his assumptions — but it will take him in the wrong direction before it takes him toward the truth.

## II. The Arc of a God's Power

> **Status: PARTIALLY RESOLVED.** The historical divergence (early gods arriving more complete), the rate of emergence declining, and the concept of conscious vs. ambient domain effects are in `wiki/concepts/decline.md` and `DECISIONS.md` (2026-05-16 — conscious vs. subconscious divine power). *Still working material:* the blooming period and its cultural apparatus (sign-watchers, naming event, false bloomings), domainless gods and their eventual exile, the plateau/long-presence dynamics.

### Early Gods vs. Modern Gods — The Historical Divergence

The oldest accounts — filtered through centuries of retelling and considerable mythologizing — describe gods arriving already in possession of their full power. The great mass migrations are spoken of in almost geological terms: gods who remade landscapes at the moment of their emergence, who spoke and cities formed, whose very presence was immediately and overwhelmingly transformative.

These accounts are probably exaggerated. But they are not entirely invented.

The most likely explanation — and one the dying deity has been circling — is that in the early period, before the proliferation of imitation gateways, gods crossed a cleaner membrane. They still lost something in transit, but less. Their arrival was more complete. Over the generations, as mortals built more imitation gateways at more sites, the membrane became progressively more permeable in the wrong direction, and gods began arriving more depleted.

This means that the historical record of divine power is also a record of the damage, though nobody reads it that way. Each generation of gods arrives a little less than the last. This is attributed to many things — the gods are becoming more humble, or more suited to the current age, or the world itself is less receptive — but never to the imitation gateways, because the imitation gateways are sacred.

**A further complication:** gods can and do actively direct the historical record about themselves. They interact with their record-keepers, shape the accounts, and over centuries of presence can revise their own emergence stories substantially. Who would disagree, or be able to disagree, over many years? This means the dying deity's investigation is working with a deeply corrupted archive — some of what he reads as primary evidence is god-directed revision. There are rumored "true" histories, suppressed or hidden accounts, but these are nearly impossible to distinguish from forgeries. The investigation is rigorous. The material it works from is not.

**What this produces culturally:** a pervasive sense of decline framed as a spiritual problem. Theologians across many traditions share a vague consensus that the world has moved away from something purer and older. They are right. They are wrong about why.

### The Rate of Emergence

Emergences have never been frequent. In the period before the current crisis, the rough average was perhaps once per decade — rare enough that each emergence was a significant event, common enough that the living memory of most societies included at least one. Gods are long-lived, with most divine presences lasting a few centuries in the mortal world; some older gods have been present for much longer, and there are wild gods rumored to be millennia old. With this lifespan, a once-per-decade emergence rate produces a world with many gods present at any given time, but not a crowded one. Each god is significant. Each emergence is remembered.

The rate has been declining. This is one of the early signs of the crisis, and one of the most discussed — fewer emergences means less renewal, means the aging population of gods is not being replenished. The last emergence before the story's present occurred approximately three decades ago. Before that, the gap had been growing. The world has been holding its breath.

### The Blooming — Powers Arriving Over Time

Modern gods — those who have emerged within living or near-living memory — arrive more depleted than their predecessors, and their powers develop gradually rather than appearing all at once. This has produced an entire cultural apparatus around the *revelation of power*: the period after emergence during which a god's domain and capabilities become apparent.

The blooming is not predictable. A god might show their first signs of power within days of emergence, or it might take years. The signs themselves are often subtle at first — an unusual quality of light around them, animals behaving strangely in their presence, a sense of profound calm or unease in people who spend time with them, small inexplicable events. Larger manifestations follow, but on no fixed schedule.

**Cultural responses to the blooming:**

Societies that receive a new god have developed complex practices around watching for and interpreting early signs:

- *Sign-watchers* — dedicated observers, often from established priestly or scholarly classes, who document everything unusual in the period following an emergence. Their records are considered sacred, debated endlessly, and frequently revised in retrospect to fit the god's eventually-revealed domain.

- *The period of ambiguity* — before a god's domain is clear, there is a socially charged window during which different factions attempt to claim the god's allegiance or interpret their signs in ways that favor their interests. A sign-watcher who records that a god seemed to calm a storm might represent the interests of sailors or farmers or merchants depending on which account becomes authoritative.

- *The naming event* — in many cultures, a god is not considered fully present until their domain is named. Before that, they are often referred to by provisional titles, location-based identifiers, or the name of their first witness. The naming event is a significant social and religious ceremony, and the authority to perform it is contested.

- *False bloomings* — events mistakenly attributed to an emerging god's power that later turn out to be coincidental. These are theologically awkward and handled differently across cultures: some acknowledge them, most quietly revise the record, a few double down and incorporate them into the doctrine.

### The Gods Without Domains

Not every god who emerges ever reveals a clear domain. These gods are waited on — sign-watchers observe them closely, sometimes desperately, for years. Sometimes domains are incorrectly assigned out of impatience or honest mistake: a god who happens to be present during a good harvest is declared a god of abundance, and the theology is built around that attribution before anyone knows whether it's true. These misassignments are difficult to correct later, especially if the god has been in a community long enough that the institutions have calcified around the misidentification.

After long enough without a manifest domain — the threshold varies by culture and circumstance — a domainless god is typically exiled. They are sent to the wild margins, to fend for themselves beyond the borders of settled life. This is framed as a spiritual matter: the god has not yet found their place, and must find it in the wilderness. In practice it is abandonment, and everyone knows it.

These exiled gods are among the most vulnerable figures in the world. They are targets — the easiest gods to kill, for those who harbor violence toward divine presence. Without a community, without followers, without the social structures that protect other gods, they have little recourse.

**The crucial exception:** some domainless gods have powers that were simply dormant — and awaken only under duress. A god attacked in the wild margins who suddenly manifests devastating defensive power is one of the more unsettling recurring stories in this world. The powers that awaken under threat tend not to be gentle. A god who discovers what they are in the moment of needing to survive it is a god without context for what they've done, and without any community to help them understand it. Some of these gods become the most dangerous wild gods in existence. Some simply disappear further into the wilderness, changed and frightened. A rare few find their way back — carrying a domain nobody expected and a history they don't know how to tell.

### The Plateau and the Long Presence

Once a god's powers have fully manifested and their domain is established, there is a period — sometimes very long, spanning generations — of relative stability. The god is present in the community, their power is known, the theological frameworks are settled. This is the period that most cultures look back on as the golden age.

Gods vary enormously in how they relate to their followers during this period. Some are deeply embedded in daily life — consulted, celebrated, present at significant events. Others are remote, their presence felt primarily through the effects of their power. Some develop close relationships with specific individuals or families; others maintain careful neutrality. Whether a god leans into the sign-reading traditions and accumulated mythology around them depends on their own nature, their relationship with their earliest followers, and whether the prestige of many followers appeals to them — and for most gods, to some degree, it does. The relationship between followers and divine power is not fully understood, but evidence suggests it is mutualistic: gods with many devoted followers seem more vital, more stable, more fully themselves. Whether this is causal or merely correlated is genuinely unclear, and the question is not asked openly, because the implications are uncomfortable in either direction.

**The long presence creates its own problems:**

- Expectations calcify. The theology that formed in the early years becomes orthodoxy, and orthodoxy becomes resistant to revision. If the god changes — and gods do change, slowly, in the mortal world — the community may be slower to notice than to defend their existing interpretation.

- Dependency grows. Societies built around a god's boon become structurally reliant on it. The farmers who have cultivated land made fertile by a god's presence may have lost the knowledge of how to farm difficult soil. The philosophical tradition sustained by a god's wisdom may have stopped generating its own thinkers.

- The god themselves may feel the weight of this. A god who has watched generations grow up in their presence, who has seen the mythology of their own emergence revised and embellished and weaponized, who knows that their people cannot fully see them — that is a specific and slow loneliness.

## III. The Decline and Its Cultural Effects

> **Status: LARGELY RESOLVED.** The texture of decline from the outside and inside, the social fracture dynamics (authority crisis, competing interpretations, anti-deity movements, migration), and the maddened gods are in `wiki/concepts/decline.md` and `wiki/societies/`. The readers of decline section (§V) has its own wiki entry. *Partial conflict check:* the "maddened god" framing in this section should be read alongside `PROJECT_PLAN.md` §4.2 which flags the "madness" framing as requiring care — the wiki entry uses more precise language.

### What Decline Looks Like From the Outside

A god's decline is not announced. It is noticed, gradually, in the texture of things. The boon begins to waver — the fertile land yields a little less, the philosophical tradition loses some of its generative quality, the healing presence is less reliably felt. People notice but do not at first name what they are noticing.

The first response, almost universally, is internal. *We have done something wrong.* The community turns inward, looking for the failure of faith or practice or virtue that has caused the god to withdraw. Ritual intensifies. Orthodoxy hardens. Dissent is suppressed as a potential cause of divine disfavor. This phase can last a very long time — generations, in some cases — during which the community becomes progressively more insular and rigid while the underlying problem continues to worsen.

**What this looks like for the protagonist's world right now:** many societies are in this phase simultaneously. The world-wide sense of spiritual crisis is being met with intensified piety, stricter orthodoxy, and increased suspicion of anyone who might be causing the gods' withdrawal. The scapegoating is widespread and the targets vary by culture — but the dynamic is consistent.

### What Decline Looks Like From the Inside

For a god, decline is a private experience that they may not fully understand. Their power becomes less reliable — sometimes it works, sometimes it doesn't, sometimes it goes wrong in ways that are frightening. Their sense of self may fragment at the edges. Some lose their domain orientation first; others retain that but lose their memory of who they were before. Some become paranoid; some become grandiose as compensation; some withdraw.

The maddened gods — those in advanced decline — have lost enough of themselves that their behavior has become erratic and sometimes violent. Their powers, no longer modulated by a coherent sense of purpose, discharge unpredictably. The communities near these gods are in genuine danger, and their responses range from continued devotion (this is a test) to organized violence (this is no longer our god). In council societies, blame may also fall on the other gods present — decline in one can be read as caused by the proximity of another, and what began as cooperation can fracture into accusation.

**A crucial ambiguity:** a god in early decline may look, from the outside, like a god in deep contemplation. The stillness and withdrawal of decline can resemble the stillness and withdrawal of great wisdom. Some of the most venerated gods in history were probably in decline and interpreted as transcendent.

### The Social Fractures

As decline spreads, the social structures built around gods begin to crack in specific ways:

**Authority crises:** If a god's power is the source of a priestly class's legitimacy, and that power is failing, the priestly class has an existential interest in denying the failure. They become enforcers of a fiction, and their increasing rigidity is the tell.

**Competing interpretations:** Decline creates a market for explanation. New theological factions emerge offering accounts of what is happening and what must be done. Some are genuine attempts to understand; many are opportunistic. The more the established orthodoxy fails, the more volatile this market becomes.

**The anti-deity movements:** As gods become dangerous or simply absent, organized movements arise that argue the gods were never worthy of the devotion given them, or that their decline proves their fundamental inadequacy, or that mortal civilization must now build something that does not depend on divine presence. These movements are right about the danger. They are often wrong about the history and the mechanism. And they struggle with the same dependency problem as everyone else — everything they know how to do was built around the gods they want to reject.

**Migration and displacement:** Societies whose patron god has declined catastrophically may lose the thing that made their location viable — the fertile land fails, the philosophical tradition collapses, the healing presence is gone. People move. They bring their theologies with them. When they settle near other communities, the theological collision is complex and sometimes violent.

## IV. The Varieties of God–Society Relationship

> **Status: RESOLVED.** All four society models (patron, council, absent-god, rejection) plus the wild margins model are in `wiki/societies/`. Specific implementations for the fragment dossier societies are in `DECISIONS.md` (2026-05-05 onwards). Use the wiki entries as canon; this section is historical context.

*A rough taxonomy — these are not fixed types but tendencies that can overlap and evolve.*

### The Patron Model
The most common. A single god is the center of a society's religious, civic, and in many cases economic life. The god's domain shapes what the society does and values. The god is present — not necessarily accessible to everyone, but present, felt, woven into the texture of daily life. Relationships between patron-model societies and their god tend to be intimate and mutually defining, which makes decline particularly devastating.

### The Council Model
Multiple gods, usually with related or complementary domains, are collectively present in a society. Rarer than the patron model, more internally complex, but in some ways more resilient — the failure of one god does not bring the whole structure down. Council societies tend to be more intellectually sophisticated about theology (having to manage multiple divine frameworks simultaneously) and more politically complex (the gods' own relationships with each other shape factional politics). They also tend to be more interesting to outside observers, which makes them targets.

The council model has a distinctive internal problem during decline: a maddened god is harder to act on when that god is also a co-sovereign. Some council societies have developed quiet mechanisms for managing this — containment, concealment, and in extreme cases, the other gods collectively moving against the declining one. These events are never discussed openly. The theological implications of gods acting against each other are too destabilizing to acknowledge.

### The Absent-God Model
A society organized around a god who has withdrawn, died, or gone wild — but whose legacy structures remain. These societies are often in a transitional crisis: the institutions are intact but the animating presence is gone. Some become rigidly traditional, maintaining the forms of devotion in the absence of the substance. Some develop toward a more philosophical relationship with their god's legacy — treating the original teachings as texts to be interpreted rather than a living presence to be consulted. A few collapse entirely.

### The Rejection Model
Societies that have actively organized against the gods — either their own former patron or divine presence in general. These range from principled philosophical movements to traumatized communities that survived a maddened god's violence. Rejection-model societies tend to be intensely communal (the anti-divine stance is a social bond), intellectually iconoclastic, and privately haunted by the question of what they lost.

## V. Signs, Portents, and the Interpretive Tradition

> **Status: PARTIALLY RESOLVED.** The readers of decline — their methodology, social position, how they find each other, how they're treated — are in `wiki/concepts/readers_of_decline.md`, developed further through Fragments 2 and 3. *Still working material:* the general sign-reading tradition (sign-watchers, naming events, false bloomings), the competitive/political dimension of sign interpretation, the mechanics of interpretive authority disputes. These will matter when the main narrative involves priestly classes or established theological institutions.

### Reading the Signs

Because a god's blooming is gradual and unpredictable, entire professional and religious traditions have grown up around the interpretation of signs. What counts as a sign, how signs are read, who has the authority to read them, and what happens when sign-readers disagree — all of this varies by culture, but the underlying practice is nearly universal.

Signs are typically:
- Environmental anomalies in proximity to the god or to a sacred site
- Behavioral changes in animals
- Unusual events in the lives of people close to the god
- Patterns in natural phenomena — weather, celestial events, the behavior of water
- Dreams and visions reported by specific individuals (whose credibility is itself contested)

**The interpretive trap:** sign-reading is inherently retrospective. A sign is recognized as significant *after* something meaningful has happened, and the sign's meaning is then read backward from the event. This creates a confirmation loop — the sign-reading tradition becomes very good at explaining what already happened, and less useful for predicting what will. But the cultural investment in it is enormous, and questioning its predictive value is theologically dangerous.

**What this means for the imitation gateways:** the construction of imitation gateways was almost certainly preceded and accompanied by an elaborate sign-reading tradition that confirmed their value. The fact that gods emerged more powerfully in the early period was read as evidence that the sacred sites were working. Every successful emergence near an established site added to the tradition's authority. The mechanism was invisible behind the confirmation.

### The Problem of Competing Signs

When two sign-readers interpret the same phenomenon differently, what happens depends entirely on their relative social power. In stable periods, sign-reading disputes are managed through established hierarchies of interpretive authority. In unstable periods — like now — they become flashpoints. A sign-reader who interprets a god's strange behavior as sacred withdrawal and one who interprets it as early decline are not having a theological dispute. They are having a political one, with real consequences for real people.

### The Readers of Decline *(the madness sign-readers)*

As gods have begun tending toward madness and decay, a distinct and controversial practice has emerged alongside the established sign-reading traditions: the reading of decline. Where ordinary sign-watchers look for evidence of a god's blooming and domain, the readers of decline attempt to reverse-engineer the same methodology — cataloguing signs not of power arriving, but of self coming apart.

The work is methodologically similar. The social position is entirely different.

Practitioners hide their faces. This is not ceremonial — it is protective. Being identified as a reader of decline is dangerous in most societies, because the act of declaring a god to be failing is itself destabilizing. The priestly classes whose authority depends on the god's continued vitality treat these readers as threats. Ordinary people, frightened and looking for causes, treat them as possible culprits. The logic is not entirely irrational: a reader of decline who declares a god to be failing *accelerates* the social fracture around that god, regardless of whether the decline was already happening. They have real power, and the power is destructive even when the diagnosis is correct.

**Whether they cause what they observe is genuinely ambiguous** — not because the decline isn't real, but because the community's response to a declaration of decline can itself harm a god whose power is partially sustained by the faith and relationship of their followers. A god who loses the confidence of their people loses something real. The readers of decline know this. Some are troubled by it. Some are not.

Different societies have settled into different uneasy accommodations:
- Some formally sanction the practice under strict conditions, treating it as a necessary institution — the way a city might employ executioners
- Some tolerate it informally, pretending not to know it exists while quietly using the information it produces
- Some actively suppress it, driving practitioners further underground and making the information harder to act on until a crisis makes suppression impossible
- A few have violently persecuted readers of decline, which has not made their gods any healthier

**How practitioners find each other** is itself a secret. There is no open school or tradition. Knowledge passes through networks built on trust and mutual need, using methods of recognition — particular phrases, objects, gestures — that function as a kind of thieves' cant. Many groups that might seem unrelated serve as unofficial support structures: traveling merchants whose routes cross many societies, certain scholarly communities, displaced people who have reason to move between cultures unseen. The current crisis has made the networks both more necessary and more dangerous. Demand has grown; so has scrutiny.

## VI. Divine Diplomacy and Inter-God Relations

> **Status: WORKING MATERIAL.** No wiki entry yet. Fragment 5 (*The City After*) touches on a catastrophic divine encounter (preservation god + incoming god in the same city), which is the closest any fragment has come to dramatizing this section. The scratchpad note about the protagonist passing through a diplomatically scarred city is still live. This section becomes load-bearing in Phases 3–5 when the protagonist begins moving through the world. Build a `wiki/concepts/divine_diplomacy.md` entry before drafting any scene involving inter-god contact or its aftermath.

### Gods as Heads of State

The more prominent and long-established gods function in some respects like sovereigns — not just of their followers, but in relation to each other. There are traditions of gods visiting one another, of delegations traveling between societies, of formal acknowledgment of each other's domains and territories. These encounters carry enormous political weight for the mortal societies involved: when a god visits another's territory, the mortals around them are acutely aware that what is happening between the gods will shape their own world.

A god who receives a visiting delegation is making a statement. A god who refuses one is making a different statement. The protocols around these visits are elaborate and culturally specific, and a misstep — by the visiting god or their mortal attendants — can have consequences that outlast the visit by generations.

### The Strangeness of Proximity

When gods are in proximity to each other, their powers interact in ways that are not fully understood and not fully controllable. The effects are varied:

- Some domains resonate constructively — a god of memory and a god of language near each other produce an environment of unusual intellectual clarity, where people find it easier to think precisely and remember accurately
- Some domains create friction — a god of fire and a god of water in close quarters produce an atmosphere of low-grade unease, small accidents, a sense of things being on the verge of going wrong
- Occasionally, the interaction produces something neither domain would produce alone — a temporary effect that belongs to neither god, that dissipates when they separate, and that sign-readers will spend years trying to categorize

Gods who travel in delegations have learned, over time, which combinations of divine presence are safe to bring where. This is itself a form of knowledge — practically valuable, jealously guarded, never fully systematized because the interactions are too variable.

### When Diplomacy Fails

Divine diplomatic incidents are among the most consequential events in the world's history. When a god's presence goes wrong in another's territory — powers destabilizing, domains colliding catastrophically, a visiting god whose decline becomes suddenly undeniable in front of witnesses — the results can be devastating and permanent. There are places in the world that bear the mark of these events: territories rendered uninhabitable, landscapes altered, entire communities destroyed in the fallout of something that began as a formal visit. These places are treated with a mixture of reverence and horror. They are not spoken about directly in polite society. Some are pilgrim sites for the morbidly curious or the theologically adventurous; most are simply avoided.

The memory of these events is part of why diplomatic contact has grown rarer. A world that once had an active network of inter-divine relations has become a world of isolated sovereigns, each managing their own crisis behind closed borders. The silence where those relationships used to be is one of the subtler signs of what is happening — felt most acutely by the gods themselves, who remember what it was like when they were not alone.

### The Decline of Divine Diplomacy

As gods decline, the apparatus of inter-divine relations has become increasingly strained. Gods who are in early decline may still maintain the forms of diplomatic engagement while becoming less predictable in their powers — a visiting god whose domain is beginning to fray is a source of anxiety for everyone around them, and the host god faces the impossible choice of whether to acknowledge what they are seeing.

Delegations have become rarer. Some gods have stopped traveling entirely. Others have stopped receiving visitors. The network of relationships that once constituted a loose kind of divine order is dissolving — each god withdrawing into their own territory, their own decline, their own increasingly isolated community of followers. The world is becoming quieter in ways that people feel without being able to name.

## VII. The Wild Gods

> **Status: LARGELY RESOLVED.** The spectrum of wild gods (almost-mythic, recently-exiled-from-decline, simply-surviving), the stigma of returned gods, and the failed-patron dynamic are all in `wiki/concepts/wild_gods.md`, developed further through Fragment 4 (*The Wild God*). *Still working material:* the domainless-god-who-awakens-under-duress (a possible figure the protagonist encounters) and the mechanics of brokered return.

### What the Margins Produce

The wild gods — those who have retreated from or been expelled from mortal society — exist across a vast spectrum. Age is the most significant variable, but it does not correlate simply with coherence or danger.

Some wild gods are almost mythic in their remove: presences felt rather than seen, associated with specific regions or natural features, providing boons or bringing disaster seemingly at random, sometimes both in the same encounter. These very old gods have been in the margins long enough that the boundary between their identity and the landscape around them has become uncertain. Whether they are still *gods* in any meaningful sense, or something the world has grown over and around, is a question without a clear answer.

Some are more recently exiled — gone wild from decline rather than from domainlessness — and retain enough of themselves to be genuinely dangerous in a directed way. A god who was once coherent, who had a domain and followers and a theology built around them, and who has lost the thread of all of that, retains the power without the modulation. These are the wild gods that mortal communities fear most, because they are recognizably *something* while being unpredictably destructive.

And some are simply surviving — domainless gods who have been in the margins for years or decades, neither thriving nor collapsing, waiting for something they cannot name.

### Returning from the Margins

It happens. Rarely, and almost never cleanly, but gods do return from the wild margins to mortal society. The circumstances vary:

Sometimes a god is displaced — their territory invaded, their followers scattered — and finds themselves in the wild not by exile but by circumstance. These gods often seek to re-enter society deliberately, and sometimes succeed, particularly if they can find a community whose need is great enough to override the stigma of a god without established provenance.

More often, return is brokered — by another god, by a mortal intermediary, by a society desperate enough to seek an unconventional solution. A community that has lost its own patron god and is watching its structures fail will consider options it would once have dismissed. The stories of these attempted returns are mixed. Many end in disaster. The despair that drives people to try anyway is real, and the rumors of successful returns — however garbled, however distorted in retelling — keep the practice alive.

**The stigma of the returned god** is significant. A god who has been in the wild carries the mark of it — in their manner, in the unpredictability of their powers, in the gaps where a history with a community should be. Building a theology around a returned god is difficult. The first witness mythology doesn't apply cleanly. The sign-reading traditions have to be improvised. The result is often an unstable, improvised theology that suits nobody perfectly and holds together primarily because the alternative is worse.

## 🗒️ Scratchpad

> **Status: WORKING MATERIAL.** Raw ideas and craft notes. Nothing here is committed. Several notes here are live for Phase 3+ planning (naming event as scene, council-model society on the protagonist's journey, domainless-god-who-awakens, the corrupted historical archive in the dying deity's flashbacks, the protagonist passing through a diplomatically scarred landscape, the once-per-decade rate and the twenty-year silence).

- The gap between early gods and modern gods is the physical record of the damage — worth thinking about how this shows up architecturally and ritually at the oldest emergence sites vs. newer ones
- The naming event as a cultural ceremony is worth developing further — it's a moment of collective interpretation that could be shown directly in a scene
- A reader of decline as a supporting character has significant potential — someone who practices a necessary and despised craft, who has genuine methodology, and who has long since made their peace with being suspected of causing what they document. Their network gives them information nobody else has. What they know about the dying deity would be dangerous.
- The council-model society is probably the most interesting to visit on the protagonist's journey — it shows what cooperation looked like at its best, and its current state of fracture shows what the decline costs at its most complex
- The absent-god model society might be where the protagonist comes from — a community organized around a god who is already gone, which would explain both her relationship to divine presence and the specific texture of her disillusionment
- The domainless god who awakens in self-defense is a complete character in a single sentence — worth keeping as a possible figure the protagonist encounters in the wild margins
- Divine diplomacy in decline: the protagonist might pass through a city bearing the permanent mark of a failed divine visit — a landscape altered, a community that has built its entire identity around surviving something that happened generations ago
- The corrupted historical archive is a structural feature of the world — every "fact" the dying deity's investigation rests on is potentially a god-directed revision. One of his flashback chapters should show this explicitly: two contradictory accounts, both plausible, and him choosing — not knowing that neither is fully true
- The dying deity witnessing an emergence and feeling the wrongness of the drain is a key flashback scene — the moment his investigation turns, and also the moment it goes subtly wrong. He feels the pull correctly. He names it incorrectly. Everything follows from that error.
- Once-per-decade emergence rate means the last god's arrival is already overdue by roughly twenty years — the world has been in an anxious, lengthening silence. This should be felt in the texture of the world the protagonist moves through.
