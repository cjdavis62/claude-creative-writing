# Vanaek

A council-society settlement. Name derives from the council-culture compound form: *van-* (crossing, threshold) + *-aek* (gathered place, center) — "place of the crossing." The name reflects Vanaek's founding logic: a site where multiple gods' domains converge, and where the social and theological work of managing that convergence takes place.

## Physical character

Vanaek's architecture integrates the divine into daily life without ceremony. Each resident god has a **domain-hall** — a building that serves as the administrative, liturgical, and residential center for that god's attendants and the practical management of their domain. Domain-halls are built into the fabric of the settlement rather than set apart: commercial buildings, taverns, and residences share walls with them. The warmth from a domain-hall's stone is a feature of the neighboring buildings.

God's marks are carved into structural joins throughout Vanaek — roof joins above doors, threshold stones, seams in load-bearing walls. The marks of each god's residency (typically three, representing the current council composition) can be read at any entrance to know which god's district one has entered. Residents do not think about the marks; they are simply how buildings are made here.

## Current status

One of Vanaek's council gods — the one whose domain is accord — is in early decline. The failure manifests as diffuse social mis-rhythms throughout the district: conversations that miss beats, small coordination failures, deals that should close and don't, the accumulated sense that the room isn't quite working. Most residents feel it without being able to name it. The attendants are aware; the god's decline has not been named publicly. A small number of attentive lay people (like Relen) are beginning to notice the pattern without yet having language for it.

## Attendant change bells

Bells mark the changing of attendants at the domain-halls at regular intervals throughout the day. These bells are woven into the rhythm of daily life in Vanaek — merchants, tavern-keepers, and long-time residents have internalized the schedule without consciously tracking it. A late bell is noticed as a slight wrongness even by those who could not tell you what they were waiting for.
