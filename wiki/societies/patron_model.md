# The Patron Model

The most common god–society relationship type. A single god is present in a community as its patron — the settlement is organized around that god's domain, which shapes its ritual life, social structure, architecture, and collective identity. The community exists, in part, as an expression of what that god makes possible.

## Characteristics

**Intimacy:** The patron model produces the deepest integration between divine and mortal life. The god is not one among several; it is the community's center of gravity. Everything — ritual cycles, seasonal calendars, the layout of civic space, what kinds of work are prestigious, how grief is processed — is calibrated around the god's presence and domain.

**Dependence:** The intimacy is also the vulnerability. A patron society's whole structure is load-bearing against the god's continued presence. The practical knowledge for how to do things without the domain is often partially or entirely lost — not through laziness, but because the domain was simply how things worked. When it fails, the infrastructure for replacement is absent.

**Ritual saturation:** The god's presence is maintained through regular communal ritual. These rituals are not purely symbolic — they are understood (correctly, at some level) as transactions: something is offered to the god's presence, something is received. The ritual structures the community's relationship to the divine around specific, legible exchange. When the exchange fails, the ritual persists as form before anyone admits the substance is gone.

**Priestly class:** Patron societies develop a priestly class whose entire identity, social position, and livelihood depends on the god's continued presence and the community's belief in that presence. This class is not villainous — they are people embedded in a structure that has real value, and whose expertise is real. But their incentives are systemically misaligned with honest diagnosis of decline. A priest who diagnoses terminal decline accurately has, in effect, announced the dissolution of everything they are.

## How decline manifests

In patron-model societies, decline is characteristically experienced as the ritual persisting in form while losing its substance. Participants return from the ceremony feeling they did it wrong — that the quality of their attention was insufficient, the words not quite right, their sincerity inadequate. The god's failure to respond is absorbed as personal failure. This is the model's cruelest feature under decline: it turns the community's awareness of the god's absence back on itself.

The priestly class typically first responds with intensification — more frequent rituals, stricter requirements for participants, higher demands for ceremonial correctness. This is not cynical; it is the natural response of people who believe the problem is inadequate performance rather than absent presence. The intensification delays diagnosis while deepening the community's sense of its own inadequacy.

**Observable decline signs (for readers of decline):**
- Rituals completing without the characteristic room-quality shift that functioning exchange produces
- Participants returning for additional attempts at the ritual (they assume they failed personally)
- Priestly anxiety masked as intensified formalism
- Young participants and the very old occasionally still receiving responses that adults do not — residual divine presence responding more easily to those less habituated to the form

## Solaven

The patron settlement featured in Fragment 3. See `wiki/locations/solaven.md`. The witness-god's domain in Solaven — the exchange of acknowledgment, the keeping of names — is an abstract domain: not elemental, not traditional. Its failure is legible only to those who know what functioning exchange feels like.

## Notes for future scenes

The patron model is the most emotionally direct setting for a scene of decline — the intimacy makes the failure personal in a way that council-model or absent-god-model scenes cannot quite reach. The protagonist's relationship to patron-model communities should be complex: she grew up in a village now probably operating as absent-god-model, which means she knows what was there and isn't. A scene in a functioning patron-model community (early in the story) and a scene in the same community under late decline (later) would do significant thematic work.
