# The Rejection Model

Societies or movements organized around the active rejection of divine presence — structured ideologically and sometimes politically against gods and god–society relationships. Not merely atheistic (indifferent to whether gods exist) but *anti-theist* in a specifically local, historically grounded sense: these communities have reasons, usually rooted in real harm, for their position.

## Origin

Rejection communities do not emerge from nowhere. They form in response to specific failures of divine presence: a patron god that declined and left the community with nothing; a council god that acted in its own interests at the community's expense; proximity-marks that drained mortals who never consented to be near the god; the priestly class's suppression of accurate decline diagnosis in order to protect its own position. The anti-deity movements the brainstorm describes have legitimate grievances rooted in real harm.

This is the most important single fact about rejection-model societies for the writer: **they are not wrong about the harm**. The harm was real. Their critique of divine presence is not a failure of understanding — it is a response to what they experienced. Where they err is in their understanding of the *mechanism*. They attribute the current crisis to gods that are dangerous and should be expelled; they do not grasp that the imitation gateways mortals built out of devotion are the actual cause of the divine plane's collapse. They are right about the symptom and wrong about the cure.

## Characteristics

**Political organization.** Unlike absent-god communities, rejection-model societies are not simply adapted to absence — they are actively organized around the position that divine presence is harmful and should be resisted. This ranges from communities that simply don't engage with god-adjacent institutions (quiet rejection) to movements that actively disrupt rituals, shelter those fleeing patron communities, or work to expose the priestly class's concealment of decline.

**Epistemological sophistication.** Rejection communities, having had to argue their position against entrenched priestly authority, often develop strong critical and evidentiary cultures. They are good at detecting hypocrisy, at demanding evidence for claims, at naming what the priestly class would rather leave unnamed. This makes them unusual allies for readers of decline — they can acknowledge and circulate accurate decline diagnosis precisely because they are not invested in the fiction of divine health. The information they carry, however, is filtered through a framework that misidentifies the cause.

**Complicated relationships to proximity-marked mortals.** A mortal who has been drained by proximity to a gateway, or permanently marked by a declining god's domain, may be welcomed in rejection communities — their experience is evidence. But the politics are complex: some rejection communities treat proximity-marks as wounds inflicted by gods (which is accurate) and their bearers as survivors; others have a more complicated relationship to those whose marks are legible signs of divine contact.

**Internal variation.** The rejection model covers significant range: a village that simply stopped performing rituals after its god left, a city with an organized philosophical tradition of anti-theism, an underground network sheltering those fleeing patron communities, an armed movement attempting to prevent new emergences. These are very different things that share a structural similarity. Not all rejection communities know or recognize each other.

## The anti-deity movements

The brainstorm names these specifically: organized resistance to the divine, operating across settlement lines, with real political power in some regions. They are right about the danger of the current moment and wrong about its cause. Their proposed solutions — preventing or reversing emergences, disrupting divine presence, dismantling the structures that sustain god–society relationships — would not address the imitation gateway problem and might accelerate the collapse by eliminating the remaining divine presence before the mechanism is understood.

This is the rejection model's tragic position: they are the most clear-eyed observers of the harm, and their proposed solutions are wrong in a way that the available evidence cannot distinguish from right.

## Relationship to readers of decline

Readers of decline and rejection communities are strange allies and strange enemies. Both observe decline accurately; both work outside the priestly mainstream. But their conclusions diverge: readers of decline are trying to understand and document what is happening, while rejection communities are trying to act on what they believe is happening. When a reader of decline's accurate diagnosis is weaponized by a rejection movement for its own purposes, the reader faces a specific kind of ethical problem: their work becomes part of an argument they did not intend to make and cannot fully endorse.

## Notes for future scenes

The protagonist is likely to encounter rejection-model communities in her journey, and the encounter should be neither dismissive nor validating. The brainstorm's note — "the anti-deity figure is right about the present danger and wrong about the mechanism" — is the key. A scene with a sympathetic, intelligent, genuinely ethical person from a rejection community who cannot be written off as a villain is one of the story's more important ethical confrontations. It forces the protagonist (and the reader) to hold two things at once: this person has been genuinely harmed, their critique of divine presence is accurate, their proposed solution would make things worse.
