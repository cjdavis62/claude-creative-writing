# The Absent-God Model

A society that was formerly organized around a patron or council god whose presence has ended — through decline, departure, or death — and has reorganized around that absence. Not a transitional state toward rejection or collapse; a stable (if altered) mode of community life that has found its own structures for meaning, continuity, and function.

## The distinction from other society types

The absent-god model is not simply a patron or council model that hasn't noticed its god is gone. The absence is known, acknowledged, and in most cases old enough to have produced a generation that has no personal memory of the god's presence. The community has adapted. What it has adapted *to* — and how — is the defining characteristic of each specific absent-god society.

The absent-god model should also not be confused with the rejection model. Rejection societies actively organize against divine presence as a political and ideological position; their absence of gods is a stance. Absent-god societies are simply communities that had a god, whose god is gone, and who have continued.

## Common features

**Preserved forms without preserved function:** Ritual structures from the god's presence often persist, transformed. The original exchange may no longer be possible, but the form of the exchange — the gathering, the words, the season — becomes a ritual of memory or community cohesion rather than divine transaction. The community may or may not understand this transformation consciously. In some absent-god societies, the ritual is performed in earnest as if the god might still answer; in others, it is preserved explicitly as tradition.

**Lost practical knowledge:** Whatever the god's domain provided — harvests, the quality of sound, the timing of community coordination — is now absent. The practical knowledge for functioning without the domain is often partially recovered (communities adapt) but rarely fully. Absent-god societies frequently have specific gaps: they are technically excellent in most areas but mysteriously poor at one thing they cannot account for. This is the domain's residue.

**Alternative meaning-structures:** Without a divine center, absent-god communities develop alternative frameworks for meaning, moral authority, and identity. These vary enormously: ancestor veneration, civic institutions, artisan traditions, relationship to land or geography. The quality of these alternatives is not uniform. Some absent-god societies are genuinely thriving; others are quietly impoverished at a level they cannot diagnose.

**A complicated relationship to the divine:** Having lived through either the god's presence (in older members) or only its aftereffects (in younger ones), absent-god communities often have a nuanced and unsentimental relationship to the idea of gods. They neither romanticize the former presence nor deny it. For some, the god's departure is experienced as abandonment; for others, as relief; for most, as simply a fact of the community's history that shapes everything without being named constantly.

## Decline within the model

Absent-god communities face a specific version of the larger crisis: they have already adapted to one absence, but their society may be subtly shaped around other, less-visible divine maintenance that is now also beginning to fail. What appears to be a stable adaptation may be partially maintained by ambient effects of gods they don't know are still (barely) present in their land. The protagonist's home village — and the larger story — is partly about what happens when those invisible stabilizers also begin to go.

## Notes for future scenes

An absent-god society is the protagonist's home register — it is what she knows as normal. The dramatic potential is in the contrast: she visits patron-model and council-model societies and perceives them as unusual, excessive, almost mythological in their divine intensity. For her, the gods' absence is simply how things are. This perception is both her wound and one aspect of the unusual clarity the dying deity reads in her.
