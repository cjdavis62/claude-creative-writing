# The Council Model

One of the four major god–society relationship types. Multiple gods — usually with related or complementary domains — are collectively present in a single settlement or region. Rarer than the patron model, more internally complex.

## Characteristics

**Resilience:** The failure of one god does not bring the whole structure down, which is the council model's primary advantage over the patron model. Other gods and their domains provide continuity even as one member declines.

**Intellectual sophistication:** Managing multiple divine frameworks simultaneously produces cultures that are more analytically careful about theology. Council-society sign-readers tend to develop finer methodological distinctions. The priestly classes are more politically complex.

**Political complexity:** The gods' own relationships with each other shape factional human politics. A god who favors a particular merchant class will have human proxies arguing their interests in civic life. When gods are in tension, their followers are in tension.

**Architectural integration:** In council settlements, domain-halls are built into the fabric of the city rather than set apart. Commercial buildings, residences, and taverns share walls with domain-halls. God's marks are incorporated into structural joins throughout the settlement (roof joins, thresholds, wall seams). The divine is woven into daily life without being ceremonially elevated.

## The internal problem under decline

A declining god in a council setting is harder to act on than in a patron society, because that god is also a co-sovereign. The council model has developed quiet mechanisms — containment, concealment, and in extreme cases collective action against a declining member — but these are never discussed openly. The theological implications of gods acting against each other are too destabilizing to acknowledge.

In practice, this produces a characteristic pattern of managed silence: the attendants know, a few attentive lay people notice, and the public fiction of a functioning council is maintained until it can no longer be.

## Attendant bells

Council settlements mark the changing of god-attendants at regular intervals throughout the day. These bells are practical (coordinating shifts in domain management) and liturgical (marking the god's continuous presence). Residents internalize the schedule without consciously tracking it. A late bell is a minor sign — noticed as wrongness before it is understood as meaning.

## Abstract domains

Not all gods in council societies have elemental or traditionally-categorizable domains. Some — particularly those associated with more intellectually sophisticated cultures — have significantly more abstract domains: accord, the quality of skilled attention, the coherence of a community's sense of itself. These are no less real than elemental domains; they are simply harder to name. Their failure is correspondingly harder to observe and diagnose: it manifests as diffuse wrongness in the texture of social life before anyone has language for it. The Vanaek accord god is the first established example of an abstract domain in the canon.

## Notes for future scenes

The council model is probably the most interesting society type to visit on the protagonist's journey — it shows what cooperation between divine presences looked like at its best, and its fracture under decline shows what the cost of the crisis is at its most complex. A scene during or after a council's quiet internal action against a declining member would be significant.
